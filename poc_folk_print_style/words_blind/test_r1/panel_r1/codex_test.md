Film A — “The Second Fire”

1. NEWCOMER

   WHO: Peter, a follower who denied being Jesus’ disciple.  
   WHAT: The risen Jesus welcomes him to a meal and entrusts him with caring for others.  
   WHY: Peter’s failure does not end his relationship with Jesus or his usefulness.

   Likely first-hearing obstacles:

   - “One night you keep replaying”: “replaying” is an idiom for repeatedly remembering.
   - Peter, Jesus and John arrive without introduction. John’s role as the writer is unclear.
   - “Denied Him”: a newcomer may not understand that Peter denied being Jesus’ follower.
   - “Went back to fishing”: could suggest Peter abandoned Jesus; the narration does not explain.
   - “The net fills”: the instruction that causes the catch is missing, so this feels abrupt.
   - “It is the Lord”: “Lord” needs connecting to Jesus.
   - “A fire of coals”: unfamiliar vocabulary; the significance takes several sentences to explain.
   - “New Testament”: assumes knowledge of the Bible’s structure.
   - “Dine,” “lovest,” “thou,” “knowest,” “thee”: unfamiliar old English. The immediate translations help with the first two lines, but Peter’s answer remains untranslated.
   - “Feed my sheep”: initially sounds like literal animal care; “my people” clarifies it.
   - “Three times, no … Three times, yes”: compresses several conversations into a pattern that takes effort to reconstruct.

2. BIBLE READER

   Yes — the repeated “fire of coals” can make a familiar restoration scene newly vivid.

3. CURIOSITY GAP

   The opening asks whether a remembered failure can be faced again. The second fire provides a satisfying answer through Peter’s story, although “Peter had one” invents his repeated remembering. **8/10.**

4. THE 5 QUESTIONS

   **Who is this for?** Someone haunted by a particular failure. The opening gives that person room to recognise themselves.

   **Main take-away?** Jesus meets Peter after his failure and still entrusts people to his care.

   **Global audience OK?** Mostly. The two fires make a strong visual connection, but the Bible terminology and numerical pattern demand concentration. At roughly 175 words, fitting reflective delivery into 60–75 seconds will be tight, especially for second-language listeners.

   **Biblically accurate?**

   - “I am not.” — exact, John 18:17 and 18:25.
   - “a fire of coals” — exact phrase in both supplied scenes.
   - “It is the Lord.” — exact, John 21:7.
   - “Come and dine.” — exact, John 21:12.
   - “Lovest thou me?” — exact wording quoted within John 21:17. However, presenting it as the complete question asked identically three times omits “Simon, son of Jonas” and the first question’s “more than these?”
   - “Lord, thou knowest all things; thou knowest that I love thee.” — exact, John 21:17.
   - “Feed my sheep.” — exact, John 21:16–17.
   - “People asked: are you with Jesus?” is rewritten dialogue, not the KJV question. It needs to be recognisable as a narrator’s summary.
   - “Peter had one” asserts that Peter kept replaying a night. His bitter weeping is supplied; recurring memories are not.
   - “At sunrise” is more specific than “when the morning was now come.”
   - “Only twice in the New Testament” cannot be established from this evidence pack’s excerpts.
   - “Fish and bread on it” places the bread on the fire; the verse explicitly places the fish there and also mentions bread.
   - “The third time, it hurts” fairly paraphrases Peter being grieved.
   - “Three times, no … Three times, yes” is a thematic summary, not literal wording of all six answers.
   - “Went back to fishing” should not imply spiritual desertion: the passage records a fishing trip without giving Peter’s motive.
   - The ending is supported by the welcome and commission. It makes no explicit universal promise.

   **ONE change that would improve it?** Replace the New Testament counting sentence with: “John uses the same words for both fires.” That preserves the discovery while making it easier to hear and grounding it in the supplied passages.

5. OVERALL: **7/10.** Memorable imagery, but the narrator explains the pattern more than the viewer needs.

Film B — “Breakfast First”

1. NEWCOMER

   WHO: Peter, a follower who failed Jesus.  
   WHAT: Jesus gives Peter food, then asks about his love and gives him responsibility.  
   WHY: Peter receives welcome and care before being asked to care for others.

   Likely first-hearing obstacles:

   - Peter and Jesus are not introduced as follower and teacher.
   - “Peter knew that kind of night”: the connection between a night and believing there is “no place for you” is indirect.
   - “I am not”: without the original question, this does not naturally answer “he did not know Him.”
   - “After Jesus rose”: “rose” may mean stood up; resurrection has not been explained.
   - “Went back to fishing”: may suggest giving up his calling.
   - “Children”: listeners may briefly imagine actual children.
   - “Ye” and “meat”: old English; modern listeners may hear meat as excluding fish. “Any food?” helps.
   - “It is the Lord!”: the speaker is unidentified, and “Lord” needs connecting to Jesus.
   - “A fire of coals”: unfamiliar vocabulary.
   - “John used”: John has not been identified as the writer.
   - “Dine,” “lovest,” “thou,” “knowest,” “thee”: the narrator translates some, but not Peter’s answer.
   - “Feed my sheep”: briefly sounds literal before “Care for my people.”
   - The final switch from feeding people spiritually to feeding Peter physically requires understanding that metaphor.

2. BIBLE READER

   Yes — the order of events, with Jesus feeding Peter before commissioning him, supplies a clear and potentially fresh observation.

3. CURIOSITY GAP

   The opening raises: “Is there still a place for someone who failed?” The meal and renewed responsibility answer it. The final sentence also gives the breakfast details a purpose. **9/10.**

4. THE 5 QUESTIONS

   **Who is this for?** Someone who believes failure has disqualified them from belonging. “You failed” is blunt; it may connect with the intended viewer, though it gives less room than A’s question.

   **Main take-away?** Jesus cares for Peter before asking Peter to care for others.

   **Global audience OK?** Mostly, and the meal gives it an accessible centre. “After Jesus rose” is a significant clarity problem. At roughly 175 words, the running time also leaves limited room for pauses and comprehension.

   **Biblically accurate?**

   - “I am not.” — exact, John 18:17 and 18:25, but its setup is mismatched: Peter is answering whether he is a disciple.
   - “Children, have ye any meat?” — exact, John 21:5.
   - “No.” — exact, John 21:5.
   - “It is the Lord!” — exact words, John 21:7; only the punctuation changes.
   - “a fire of coals” — exact phrase from both supplied scenes.
   - “Come and dine.” — exact, John 21:12.
   - “Lovest thou me?” — exact excerpt, with the same omission of the fuller questions as A.
   - “Lord, thou knowest all things; thou knowest that I love thee.” — exact, John 21:17.
   - “Feed my sheep.” — exact, John 21:16–17.
   - “Peter knew that kind of night” implies Peter felt he had no place left. The supplied text establishes grief, not that belief.
   - “Three times said he did not know Him” is not what the supplied John passages explicitly report. “Three times denied Him” would be more faithful to this evidence.
   - “At sunrise” adds precision beyond “morning.”
   - “Went back to fishing” supplies no evidence that Peter had abandoned his calling.
   - The shared fire wording, distribution of bread and fish, and conversation after the meal are supported.
   - “Jesus still had work for him” is a fair inference from the commission. The closing sentence is earned by the sequence of the passage.
   - No explicit over-promise: the ending describes what Jesus did for Peter.

   **ONE change that would improve it?** Change “After Jesus rose” to “After Jesus rose from the dead.” A newcomer needs that fact stated plainly.

5. OVERALL: **8/10.** The story has a clearer emotional destination, and its final line makes the meal matter.

WHICH FILM WINS: **Film B.** Its ending grows directly from an action the viewer has just witnessed: Jesus giving food.  
A’s fire connection is memorable; B makes grace easier to understand and feel on one hearing.

TOP 3 FIXES

1. Remove the invented inner history: “Peter had one” and “Peter knew that kind of night.” Let his documented denial and grief establish the connection.
2. Make the essential facts clear: identify Peter as Jesus’ follower, state the resurrection explicitly, and describe his denial in language that fits “I am not.”
3. Create breathing room for a global audience. Cut explanatory repetition and trivia so the meal, Peter’s answer and the ending can land within 60–75 seconds.
