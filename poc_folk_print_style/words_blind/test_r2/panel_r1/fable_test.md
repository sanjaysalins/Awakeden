# Cold test — Film C and Film D ("The Second Fire", John 21:1-17)

Both films share the same first 85%. Only the last narrator turn differs. Shared notes are given under Film C and marked "same" under Film D.

---

## FILM C (ends on the meal)

### 1. Newcomer (no Bible knowledge, English as a second language)

- **WHO:** Peter, a friend of Jesus, who said three times that he did not know Him.
- **WHAT:** After Jesus rises from the dead, He meets Peter on a beach, cooks him breakfast, asks "do you love me?" three times, and gives him a job.
- **WHY:** The friend who failed is fed first and trusted again. Failure was not the end.

A newcomer gets all three. The "why" is shown, not said, so a weak listener may only half get it.

**Words and lines a newcomer would NOT get on first hearing:**

- "replaying" — not a common word for ESL listeners. "Keep thinking about" is safer.
- "The night Jesus was arrested" — who is Jesus, and why arrested? No setup. Survivable, but cold.
- "followers" — fine for most, slightly modern (social media sense may confuse).
- "Peter went back to fishing" — we were never told he was a fisherman. "Back" to what?
- "It fills." — with what? The word "fish" is never said here. The picture must carry it.
- "It is the Lord." — who is "the Lord"? Never told that the Lord = Jesus. Also nobody is named as the speaker; it sounds like the narrator.
- "a fire of coals" — "coals" is not a common word.
- "those exact words appear only twice" — "appear" is fine; the idea is clear.
- "This one has fish and bread." — "this one" = this fire. Easy to miss by ear. Sounds like "this Bible" or "this time".
- "Come and dine." — "dine" is old; glossed at once ("Come and eat"). Good.
- "Lovest thou me?" — glossed at once. Good.
- "Peter is hurt." — real risk. He just jumped into the sea. An ESL listener can hear "hurt" as injured. "Sad" is safer.
- "thou knowest all things; thou knowest that I love thee" — long old-English line, but glossed straight after. Acceptable.
- "Feed my sheep." — glossed ("Take care of my people"). Good.
- "Three times, no, at the first fire. Three times, yes, at the second." — clear and strong, IF they caught the fire fact earlier.

### 2. Bible reader

**Yes.** Most readers know the three-and-three. Fewer have noticed "a fire of coals" sits at both scenes, and fewer still have put it as "before Jesus asked Peter to feed anyone, Jesus fed Peter." That last line is a real gift.

### 3. Curiosity gap

- **Question opened:** "Is there one night you keep replaying?" — it promises something about MY night, and about getting free of it.
- **Second gap, mid-film:** "those exact words appear only twice" — where is the other one? Paid in the very next sentence. Too fast to build any pull, but honest.
- **Paid off?** Half. Peter's night is answered. The viewer's night is never touched again. The last line is about the meal, not about the night, so the opening and the ending do not shake hands.
- **Score: 6/10.**

### 4. The 5 questions

**Who is this for?** Someone carrying one bad failure. The hook finds them. The ending talks about Peter's job, not their night, so it half leaves them.

**Main take-away:** Jesus feeds you before He asks anything of you. Clear, earned, and true to the text (v13 before v15).

**Global audience OK?** Mostly yes. Good habit of glossing every KJV line straight after. Trouble spots: "replaying", "hurt", "this one", "It fills", "the Lord" never explained.

**Biblically accurate? Quote check, word for word:**

- "I am not." — exact (John 18:17, 18:25). PASS.
- "It is the Lord." — exact (21:7). PASS. Note: spoken by "that disciple whom Jesus loved", not the narrator and not Peter. Unattributed here. Not wrong, slightly loose.
- "a fire of coals" — exact (21:9 and 18:18). PASS.
- "Come and dine." — exact (21:12). PASS.
- "Lovest thou me?" — exact as in 21:17. PASS. Note: the first time is really "lovest thou me more than these?" The film says He asks this three times, which trims the first. Fair shortening, not a misquote.
- "Lord, thou knowest all things; thou knowest that I love thee." — exact (21:17). PASS.
- "Feed my sheep." — exact (21:16, 21:17). PASS. (First reply is "Feed my lambs" — not claimed otherwise.)

**Claims to flag:**

1. **"In the whole Bible, those exact words appear only twice."** The pack only proves the two places. I believe it holds for the KJV English, but "the whole Bible" is a bigger claim than this pack can prove, and it is only true of one translation's wording. Safer and still strong: "In the whole New Testament" or "John uses those words only twice." This is the one factual line I would have checked by a full-text search before lock.
2. **"Peter stood by a fire... Three times, no, at the first fire."** In John, the first "I am not" (18:17) is at the door, to the girl who kept it. The fire comes in 18:18. Two of the three are plainly at the fire. "At the first fire" is a small squeeze. Most viewers will never mind; a careful reader will.
3. **"Three times, yes, at the second."** Peter says "Yea, Lord" twice. The third time he does not say "Yea" — he says "thou knowest all things." And the text does not say they were still at the fire. Fair reading, but it is a reading. The text itself never says the three questions answer the three denials. It is offered in the narrator's voice, not as Scripture, so it passes.
4. **"Peter went back to fishing."** Text: "I go a fishing." "Went back" hints he gave up. The text does not say why. Mild.
5. **"People asked"** — a door-girl, then "they". Fine.

**Unfair picture of Peter?** No. He is not mocked. His weeping is left out, which keeps guilt low. One risk: "Three times no / three times yes" can be heard as Peter paying back his debt, one yes per no. That leans toward "he earned it back." The last line ("Jesus fed Peter" first) corrects it. Good save.

**Over-promise?** None. Nothing is promised to the viewer at all.

**Best version possible? ONE change:** Make the last line reach back to the opening word "night." Right now the film opens on a night and closes on a meal.

### 5. Score: 7.5 / 10

## FILM D (ends on the fire)

### 1. Newcomer

- **WHO:** Same — Peter, the friend who said no three times.
- **WHAT:** Same — beach, breakfast, three questions, a job.
- **WHY:** Jesus came and found Peter at the same kind of place where he failed, fed him, and trusted him.

**Words and lines a newcomer would NOT get:** all of the Film C list down to "Feed my sheep", plus:

- **The three questions are never explained.** "He asks Peter three times" and "The third time, Peter is hurt" are left hanging. Why three? Why is he hurt? Film C answers this. Film D does not. A newcomer may think Jesus is being unkind, or did not believe him.
- "Then He gave him work." — what work? "Take care of my people" is one line back, so most will join it up.
- "Jesus met him at another one." — "another one" = another fire. Clear enough by ear because "fire" is in the sentence before.

### 2. Bible reader

**Yes, a little less.** The fire fact is still there. The "fed him first, then gave him work" line keeps the best insight in six words. But the three-and-three is dropped, so a reader may feel the obvious thing was left unsaid.

### 3. Curiosity gap

- **Question opened:** same — the night you keep replaying.
- **Paid off?** Better than C. "Peter's worst night happened by a fire. Jesus met him at another one." The word "night" comes back. The title is paid. The viewer can put their own night in Peter's place without being told to.
- **But:** a new small gap is opened and never closed — why three times, why hurt.
- **Score: 7/10.**

### 4. The 5 questions

**Who is this for?** Same viewer, and this ending speaks to them more directly. "Worst night" is their word.

**Main take-away:** Jesus comes to the place of your failure and meets you there. Slightly softer on "fed before asked", stronger on "He came to find him."

**Global audience OK?** Same trouble spots as C. The closing lines are the plainest English in either film — short words, short sentences. Very good.

**Biblically accurate?** All seven quotes are the same as Film C — all exact. Same flags 1, 2, 4, 5. Flag 3 does not apply (the "three times yes" line is gone). New points:

- **"Peter's worst night"** — the text never ranks his nights. It is a fair human judgment (Luke 22:62, "wept bitterly"), said in the narrator's voice, not as Scripture. Passes, but it is a feeling the film supplies.
- **"Jesus met him at another one"** — true to 21:9. Jesus is already on the shore with the fire made. Earned.
- **"Then He gave him work"** — true to 21:15-17. Plain and safe.

**Unfair picture of Peter?** No. And it avoids the "three yeses pay off three noes" risk completely. This is the more grace-clean ending.

**Over-promise?** None.

**Best version possible? ONE change:** Add one short line that explains the three questions, so "three times" and "hurt" are not left hanging. For example, before the close: "Three times he had said no. Three times, he gets to say: I love you."

### 5. Score: 7 / 10

---

## WHICH FILM WINS

**Film C wins as written, narrowly (7.5 vs 7)** — it is the only one that closes the "three times / Peter is hurt" thread, and its final line is the single best line in either film.
**But Film D has the better LAST line for the hurting viewer** — it returns to the "night" of the opening. The best film is C's three-and-three plus D's close. Neither is that film yet.

## TOP 3 FIXES (across both, most important first)

1. **Merge the endings.** Keep the three-and-three (C), keep "Jesus fed him first. Then He gave him work" (D), and END on "Peter's worst night happened by a fire. Jesus met him at another one." That pays the opening, the title, the fire fact, and the three questions in one close. Word the three-and-three so it is a gift, not a payback (e.g. "three times, he gets to say I love you").
2. **Fix or verify the "whole Bible" claim.** Run a full-text search of the KJV for "fire of coals" before lock, or narrow the line to "the whole New Testament." Also soften "at the first fire" for the denials, since John puts the first one at the door — "by that fire" or "that night" avoids the squeeze.
3. **Clear four ear-traps for the newcomer:** "Peter is hurt" → "Peter is sad" (he just jumped in the sea; "hurt" sounds like injured); "It fills" → "It fills with fish"; "This one has fish and bread" → "On this fire: fish and bread"; and tell us once that "the Lord" is Jesus (e.g. "One of them says: 'It is the Lord.' It is Jesus."). Consider "keep thinking about" for "replaying."
