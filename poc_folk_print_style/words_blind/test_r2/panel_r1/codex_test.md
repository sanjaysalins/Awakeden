## Film C

**1. NEWCOMER**

**WHO:** Peter, who denied being a follower of Jesus, and Jesus, who has risen from the dead.  
**WHAT:** Jesus meets Peter after an unsuccessful fishing trip, feeds him, asks about his love, and entrusts him with caring for others.  
**WHY:** Peter’s failure has not ended his relationship with Jesus or his ability to help others.

Likely first-hearing obstacles:

- **“Jesus,” “Peter,” “His followers”** — their relationship becomes clear, but why following Jesus was dangerous does not.
- **“He rose from the dead”** — understandable words, but an enormous event delivered too quickly to absorb.
- **“It is the Lord”** — newcomers may not immediately understand that “the Lord” means Jesus.
- **“A fire of coals”** — unfamiliar wording for a charcoal fire.
- **“Those exact words appear only twice”** — the significance of repeated wording needs processing while the story continues.
- **“Come and dine,” “Lovest thou me,” “thou knowest,” “thee”** — unfamiliar older English. The immediate translations help.
- **“Feed my sheep” / “Take care of my people”** — the explanation helps, but viewers must switch from literal fish and food to a metaphor.
- **“Three times, yes”** — we hear only one answer, so the viewer must reconstruct the other two.
- **“The first fire” / “the second”** — requires remembering the opening scene.

**2. BIBLE READER**

**Yes:** The connection between the two charcoal fires, followed by the observation that Jesus feeds Peter before commissioning him, offers a worthwhile fresh reading.

**3. CURIOSITY GAP**

The opening suggests: **Can someone return from a failure they cannot stop remembering?** The meal and renewed responsibility answer that through Peter’s story. The closing sentence makes the answer emotionally clear. **8/10.**

**4. THE 5 QUESTIONS**

- **Who is this for?** Someone ashamed of betraying or abandoning another person, especially someone who fears rejection.
- **Main takeaway?** Jesus gives Peter food and renewed responsibility after his denial. Care comes before the commission.
- **Global audience OK?** Mostly. The concrete pictures help. The older English, repeated translations, and rapid transitions demand considerable attention.
- **Biblically accurate?**
  - **Exact KJV excerpts:** “I am not”; “It is the Lord”; “a fire of coals”; “Come and dine”; “Lovest thou me?”; “Lord, thou knowest all things; thou knowest that I love thee”; and “Feed my sheep” all match the supplied wording.
  - **One direct-speech problem:** “People asked: are you one of His followers?” is not KJV wording. Its introduction presents it as spoken dialogue, rather than clearly as a summary.
  - The narrator’s modern explanations are paraphrases, not KJV quotations. Their placement generally makes that distinction understandable.
  - The first question also includes **“more than these.”** The script quotes an exact excerpt but compresses the differences between the questions.
  - **“In the whole Bible … only twice”** cannot be established from this evidence pack. The supplied passages establish two occurrences in John.
  - **“Three times, no, at the first fire”** overstates the supplied locations: the first denial is at the door, before the fire is introduced.
  - **“Peter went back to fishing”** can suggest spiritual retreat. The passage records his fishing decision without supplying that motive.
  - **“Sunrise”** is more specific than “morning”; **“His friend”** is a relational description not explicitly supplied here.
  - The three-denials/three-answers connection is a reasonable interpretation, but the passage does not explain it as a deliberate exchange of three failures for three affirmations.
  - No explicit over-promise. The script stays with Peter rather than guaranteeing that every damaged human relationship will be restored.
- **ONE change that would improve it most?** Replace “Three times, no, at the first fire. Three times, yes, at the second” with **“Peter had denied Jesus three times.”** That fixes the location claim, reduces repetition, and gives the excellent final sentence room.

**5. OVERALL: 8/10**

## Film D

**1. NEWCOMER**

**WHO:** Peter, who denied following Jesus, and Jesus, who returns after rising from death.  
**WHAT:** Jesus feeds Peter and gives him responsibility, in a scene linked to the fire near Peter’s denial.  
**WHY:** Jesus still welcomes and trusts someone who failed him.

Likely first-hearing obstacles:

- **Jesus, Peter, and following Jesus** — their relationship is introduced, but the danger behind Peter’s denial remains unexplained.
- **“He rose from the dead”** — a major event passed over very quickly.
- **“The Lord”** — its identification with Jesus may not be immediate.
- **“A fire of coals”** — unfamiliar expression.
- **“Those exact words appear only twice”** — viewers must understand a textual observation while following the action.
- **“Come and dine,” “Lovest thou me,” “thou knowest,” “thee”** — the translations help, but add another listening step.
- **“Feed my sheep” / “my people”** — the metaphor and the people concerned need interpretation.
- **“Then He gave him work”** — newcomers could picture employment rather than responsibility for people.
- **“Another one”** — understandable if the viewer has retained the opening fire; otherwise the ending loses its connection.

**2. BIBLE READER**

**Yes:** The repeated charcoal-fire setting can bring a familiar passage into sharper focus, although the final observation offers less than C’s feeding-before-commissioning connection.

**3. CURIOSITY GAP**

The opening suggests: **What happens when Jesus meets someone who denied him?** The story answers with a meal and responsibility. Returning to the fire provides visual closure, but the final sentence leaves more of the emotional meaning for viewers to infer. **7/10.**

**4. THE 5 QUESTIONS**

- **Who is this for?** Someone haunted by failure who needs evidence that they can still be welcomed.
- **Main takeaway?** Jesus meets Peter again and gives him a continuing place in his work.
- **Global audience OK?** Mostly, with the same language and pacing difficulties as C. The final fire image is visually accessible, provided its significance has registered.
- **Biblically accurate?**
  - **Exact KJV excerpts:** “I am not”; “It is the Lord”; “a fire of coals”; “Come and dine”; “Lovest thou me?”; Peter’s full answer; and “Feed my sheep” all match the supplied text.
  - **“Are you one of His followers?”** is non-KJV direct speech. The narrator’s explanatory translations are also paraphrases, though more clearly presented as explanations.
  - As in C, the first question’s **“more than these”** is omitted.
  - The whole-Bible occurrence claim exceeds what the supplied evidence establishes.
  - “Went back to fishing” risks implying abandonment of discipleship; the passage supplies no motive. “Sunrise” and “friend” are additional descriptive choices beyond the supplied wording.
  - **“Peter’s worst night”** is an unsupported ranking of his experience. The evidence records denial and bitter weeping, not Peter identifying this as his worst night.
  - **“Then He gave him work”** reasonably summarizes the commission, but makes it sound more transactional than the passage’s language about caring for sheep.
  - No explicit over-promise. However, the sequence “fed him first … gave him work” could leave a vulnerable viewer hearing an expectation to repay kindness.
- **ONE change that would improve it most?** Replace **“Peter’s worst night happened by a fire”** with **“Peter had denied Jesus beside a fire.”** It preserves the visual ending and removes the unsupported superlative.

**5. OVERALL: 7/10**

**WHICH FILM WINS**

**Film C.** Its final sentence gives the intended viewer a clearer reason for hope: Jesus cares for Peter before entrusting him with caring for others.  
D closes the visual pattern, but “gave him work” weakens the warmth, and “worst night” claims more than the evidence supplies.

**TOP 3 FIXES**

1. **Tighten textual accuracy:** correct the non-KJV direct question, narrow the whole-Bible claim, and remove C’s placement of all three denials at the fire.
2. **Keep C’s final sentence as the landing.** It communicates grace most clearly; D’s “work” and “worst night” wording weaken that effect.
3. **Make room to listen.** Both scripts contain about 190 spoken words. At 60–75 seconds, three voices, translations, and emotional pauses will feel crowded. Cut repeated explanation and allow the ending a pause.
