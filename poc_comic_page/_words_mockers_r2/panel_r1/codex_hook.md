## PER VERSION

**X — 8/10**

- **First five seconds:** “A king named David wrote these words. A thousand years later…” establishes a person and an intriguing time gap. But “these words” has no referent yet. The card promises a mystery; narration initially supplies background.
- **Pacing:** Best of the three. The paired quotations arrive with little interference, “Deliver means rescue” removes a comprehension barrier, and “He could have” creates a second hook after the verbal resemblance pays off.
- **Weakest ten seconds:** The closing stretch beginning “But David’s song…” The dramatic scene gives way to explanatory narration and a predictable invitation. Some viewers will recognize the conclusion and leave before hearing it.
- **Odds of watching to the end:** **49% ±15 percentage points.** Its main advantage is forward movement; its main liability is announcing the resemblance before viewers discover it.

**Y — 7/10**

- **First five seconds:** “A king named David wrote the words. The crowd at the cross…” gets to the collision faster than X. Still, “the words” asks viewers to care about something they have not heard.
- **Pacing:** Strongest scene orientation, weakest economy. “First, David,” “Now the cross,” and the rescue definition help an unfamiliar audience follow. But “Hear it? Almost the same words, a thousand years apart” repeats the opening immediately after the quotations have demonstrated it. You spend the reveal twice.
- **Weakest ten seconds:** The passage beginning “Hear it?” through the next taunt. The narrator interrupts the confrontation to explain the obvious, then announces another line before allowing it to happen. This is where momentum becomes instruction.
- **Odds of watching to the end:** **45% ±15 percentage points.** Clear enough to retain newcomers, but repeated signposting consumes scarce seconds.

**Z — 5/10**

- **First five seconds:** “King David wrote these words. A crowd at a cross…” is compact, but “a crowd at a cross” sounds deliberately nonspecific. The setup feels withheld rather than mysterious.
- **Pacing:** The full “If thou be the Son of God” taunt adds useful confrontation. Almost everything around it is overmanaged: “First,” “Then,” “They said more,” “Jesus had said,” “Now the ending.” These are instructions for following a presentation, not dramatic movement. The isolated John quotation also introduces an unexplained “it.”
- **Weakest ten seconds:** “That means people like you… No one is pushing you. The invitation is open.” This piles reassurance onto an ending that has already arrived. “No one is pushing you” introduces the idea of pressure while the viewer is deciding whether to leave.
- **Odds of watching to the end:** **35% ±15 percentage points.** More complete quotations do not compensate for the repeated pauses and extended landing.

These are subjective forecasts for broadly distributed viewers who reach the narration, assuming comparable execution. The ranges express uncertainty, not measured statistical error bars. Audience targeting, delivery, and editing could reverse X and Y.

## RANKING

**X > Y > Z.** X moves most efficiently from resemblance to confrontation to voluntary sacrifice; Y explains too often; Z narrates its own structure and prolongs its farewell.

## WHAT TO TAKE FROM THE OTHERS

Use **X as the base**. Borrow Y’s clear identification that David is recording mockery: otherwise, the David voice can sound as though David himself is taunting someone. Borrow Z’s fuller conditional taunt, which makes the challenge to Jesus immediately intelligible. Keep X’s short final invitation.

## ANY ACCURACY PROBLEM

- **Z: “Remember and turn unto the LORD.”** The supplied verse says people “**shall remember and turn**.” Removing “shall” turns a prediction into an apparent command. That is a materially misleading quotation.
- **Y: “The crowd at the cross spoke them.”** This initially claims identical wording; the supplied quotations differ. X and Z qualify the resemblance.
- **All three: “He could have [come down].”** John 10 supports voluntary self-giving, but does not explicitly state this counterfactual. Present it as theological inference, not something the quoted line directly establishes.
