Naaman in the Jordan — Visual Design Brief

Fable's plan for the pictures. 6 story pages + a front and back cover, matched beat-for-beat to the locked narration (69s).

The one big call: the front cover shows Nazareth

The hook says: "Jesus told his hometown one old story — and they tried to throw him off a cliff."

If the cover showed Naaman's river instead, you'd be looking at the wrong picture while hearing about a cliff and an angry crowd. So the front cover shows Jesus on the cliff edge, the crowd behind him — picture matches sound. The back cover swings back to Naaman at the river, so both halves of the story still get a cover each.

Who's in it

Jesus — unchanged. Same face/build as every other episode, no redesign.

Naaman — a heavily-built Syrian commander in his late 40s, weathered, bearded, in bronze armor and a scarlet cloak. His leprosy is shown as faint pale dry patches on one forearm only — never his face, never a wound — and they vanish completely at the healing page.

The little maid — the servant girl who first points him to the prophet. Appears once only, one panel, no repeat.

Naaman's servants — three men, appear once (the "wash and be clean" page).

Elisha — never shown, on any page. "He didn't even come out" is the whole point — the shut door speaks for him.

The Nazareth crowd — drawn as one pressing mass of ink, no single face finished or recognizable — anger shown in posture, not faces.

The 8 pages

FRONT COVER — Nazareth, the cliff brow

Jesus standing at the cliff edge, the crowd pressing behind him, storm light. Title: "ONE OLD STORY" · 2 KINGS 5

F01 — A great general, and a leper

Naaman alone at dawn, looking at the patches on his own arm. The maid points, off in a small panel.

Stain: building Swirl: none yet

F02 — The shut door

Naaman's grand chariot outside a plain house. The door stays shut. A messenger points to the river instead.

Stain: holding Swirl: first trace

F03 — The fury

Naaman storms away in profile, back to the shut door, toward his chariot. His own KJV outburst as the caption.

Stain: peak Swirl: small, behind him

F04 — The servants

His own men stop him on the road, hands open. His head just beginning to bow. The KJV line "Wash, and be clean."

Stain: turning Swirl: ahead of him now

F05 — The seventh wash

Naaman rises from the Jordan, both arms out — skin completely clean, no trace of the patches. His armor left on the bank.

Stain: gone (one pale ring left) Swirl: rises above him

This is the healing page — where the "living ink" finally outweighs the "stain," right on the moment he's made new.

F06 — Nazareth, the telling

Jesus in the synagogue, hand on the scroll, telling this exact story. The crowd rising, angry. The KJV line: "saving Naaman the Syrian."

Stain: none (this isn't an uncleanness page) Swirl: strongest point in the episode

BACK COVER — the plain river

Naaman small in the wide river at dawn, healed, his armor piled bigger than he is on the bank. Title: "STILL WORTH LOSING" · LUKE 4:27

4 small calls I'm making by default (say if you want it different)

1. Back cover title wording. Going with: "STILL WORTH LOSING" (the narration's own closing line) rather than the more literal "SAVING NAAMAN THE SYRIAN" — the landing line is what a cover should carry, not a caption.

2. How angry does page F03 (the fury) get? The design allows the maximum on the "how much unusual ink on one page" rule — right at the ceiling, not over it. Going with: allow it (it's legal, and it's the angriest page on purpose). Say if you'd rather play it safer.

3. Show his leprosy as literal skin patches, or fold it entirely into the paper-stain effect? Going with: both — faint patches on his arm (so "new skin, like a child's" has something real to show disappearing) plus the paper-stain effect underneath. A leaner version would drop the literal patches and rely on the stain effect alone.

4. Does the angry crowd need its own reference image? Since no single face in the crowd is ever drawn clearly, Fable's case is that there's nothing for a likeness to drift on. Going with: no ref, save the cost — cheap insurance (cropping one from the cover) is there if a crowd render ever looks off.

Full page-by-page prose (character builds, exact panel/caption text, full ink-dosage detail) is with me — ready to turn into the real production script once you say go.
