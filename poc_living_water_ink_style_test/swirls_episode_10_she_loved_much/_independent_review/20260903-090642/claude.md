# Independent review — claude (OK, 170s)

Cross-checked every quoted clause against Luke 7:36-50 KJV. Findings below.

**KJV verbatim — clean.** Every word inside quotation marks matches KJV exactly, including the two elided joins (marked correctly with "…"):
- v37b/38 splice: "A woman in the city, which was a sinner… stood at his feet…" — verbatim on both sides of the ellipsis.
- v39 splice: "This man, if he were a prophet, would have known… for she is a sinner." — verbatim.
- v42, v43, v44, v47, v49, v50 quotes — each checked word-for-word, all exact. No altered words found.

**Doctrine on the exact risk flagged in the brief (forgiveness-earned-by-love) — handled correctly.** The piece sequences the debt parable ("when they had nothing to pay, he frankly forgave them both" → "which of them will love him most?" → "he, to whom he forgave most") *before* the "for she loved much" quote, then states the resolution explicitly: "Forgiven first — nothing to pay. Love came after: the receipt, not the price." That's the right evangelical reading (love as evidence, not cause) and it primes the listener correctly before the ambiguous KJV "for" lands. The close — "Not thy tears. Not thy ointment. Thy faith." — correctly isolates faith, not works, as what saved her. No fear/gain-loss framing anywhere. This is sound.

**Clarity break: the alabaster box callback is unearned.** The closing line "She came in carrying an alabaster box. She left carrying his peace." is the piece's whole bookend device — but "alabaster box" is never mentioned anywhere earlier in the piece. It sat inside the elided part of the opening quote ("…" swallows v37's "brought an alabaster box of ointment"). A listener with zero Bible knowledge hits that final line with no idea what box is being referred to — it's a callback to information the piece itself never gave them. Either name the box in the opening quote/description, or rebuild the bookend on a word actually established in the piece (e.g. "ointment," which IS stated).

**Hook — scored 6/10.** "The man who invited Jesus to dinner gave him nothing a host owes. No water for his feet. No kiss. No oil. The woman nobody invited brought all three." It's not a template opener (no "Did you know/Imagine"), and the staccam triplet is a nice technique — but the entry point is etiquette/hospitality-norms, which is an intellectual hook, not a visceral one. For a cold scroller with no investment in Bible content, this needs the human scandal up front (an uninvited "sinner" crashing a Pharisee's dinner and making a scene) rather than leading with what the host failed to provide. Under 8, so this alone forces the verdict below.

**Landing — scored 8/10.** Strong: built from the piece's own image/verb (alabaster box / faith / peace), passes the "could this end a different piece?" test — it couldn't. This matches the pattern already validated in this project (concrete word from the piece's own text over an abstract CTA phrase). The only thing holding it below 9 is the unearned "alabaster box" reference above. Note: the piece has zero direct second-person address to the listener anywhere — worth confirming that's the deliberate house style (letting the narrative itself be the invitation) rather than a missing CTA beat, given the project's stated model is "CTA-to-Jesus."

No invented doctrine, no contrarian reading, no thread-swapping — the host/love/forgiveness/faith spine runs clean from hook to landing.

VERDICT: REVISE
TOP FIXES:
1. Sharpen the hook — open on the woman's scandal/social risk (uninvited "sinner" at a Pharisee's table), not host-etiquette failures; current opener is intriguing but not a visceral scroll-stopper (6/10).
2. Fix the alabaster-box callback — it's never established earlier in the piece (elided by the opening "…"); either surface it in the opening quote or rebuild the closing bookend on "ointment," which is actually stated.
3. Confirm the fully indirect, no-second-person landing is an intentional house-style choice, not a missing CTA beat, given the series' own CTA-to-Jesus model.
