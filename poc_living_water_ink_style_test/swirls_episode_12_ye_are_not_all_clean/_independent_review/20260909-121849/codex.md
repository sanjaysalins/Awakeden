# Independent review — codex (OK, 116s)

**HOOK: 9/10.** “Judas walked out into the night on feet Jesus had washed” is immediate, concrete, and arresting. However, its strongest detail is an inference rather than an explicit statement.

**LANDING: 5/10.** “Sit down. Let Him wash your feet too” uses the piece’s central image, but the script has made that image logically unstable: Judas’s feet were apparently washed, yet Jesus still called him “not clean.” The CTA therefore cannot clearly mean salvation, repentance, forgiveness, or sanctification.

Specific findings:

- **The central inference is presented as narrated fact.** John says Jesus “washed their feet” while Judas was present, so Judas’s inclusion is reasonable. But Scripture never individually describes Jesus washing Judas, kneeling “at that very foot,” or doing so “minutes before.” The lines “feet Jesus had washed,” “Jesus had knelt at that very foot,” and “He washed that heel anyway” turn a defensible inference into cinematic certainty.

- **The governing metaphor contradicts the CTA.** The narration emphasizes that Judas was “washed” but “not clean.” It then warns the listener that they may likewise be “serving, washed, and already…turning toward the door,” only to prescribe another washing: “Let Him wash your feet too.” On first hearing, the obvious question is: *Why would that save or restore me when it did not make Judas clean?* The piece never distinguishes physical foot-washing, Christ’s saving cleansing, and ongoing sanctification.

- **“Was never only for Peter” asserts a contested interpretation.** “If I wash thee not, thou hast no part with me” was spoken directly to Peter. It may symbolize the cleansing Christ provides, but “was never only for Peter” requires argument the narration does not supply. The text immediately distinguishes one already washed and clean from the washing of his feet; that distinction is precisely what the script needs to explain before making this its gospel invitation. See the sequence in [John 13:8–11 KJV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John+13%3A1-30&version=KJV).

- **“He hasn’t put it away” invents an ongoing basin theology.** Literally, Jesus had already finished and “was set down again.” Poetically, the line implies that Christ is still offering the same washing represented by the basin, but John’s text does not make that claim. It is evocative, not exegetically established.

- **The warning is ominous but spiritually vague.** “Maybe you have sat at this table for years too—serving, washed, and already, quietly, turning toward the door” casts the listener as a possible Judas without identifying the sin, calling for repentance, or anchoring the appeal in Christ’s finished saving work. It creates dread about hidden apostasy more readily than grace-anchored conviction.

- **The sop is overinterpreted.** “He put the bread in that hand anyway” frames the sop as an obvious act of offered mercy. In John, giving the sop directly answers the question of who will betray Jesus. It may carry hospitality symbolism, but the narration cannot safely make “anyway” bear that theological weight without qualification.

- **The spine changes in the final movement.** It begins with Christ’s knowing service toward His betrayer, moves into a warning that the listener may secretly resemble Judas, then lands on Peter’s statement about receiving Christ’s washing. Those are related themes, but the missing distinction between Judas and Peter prevents them from becoming one coherent argument.

- **KJV quotation check: pass.** Every word placed inside quotation marks appears verbatim in the KJV passage, including quoted fragments that omit surrounding words such as “now,” “and,” or “For.” The wording matches [John 13:1–30 KJV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John+13%3A1-30&version=KJV). “Already” is outside quotation marks; the KJV itself says “having now put.”

VERDICT: REVISE
TOP FIXES:
1. Distinguish Judas’s physically washed feet from the saving cleansing signified in Jesus’s words, so the CTA does not prescribe what Judas already received.
2. Remove or qualify invented certainties such as “knelt at that very foot,” “minutes before,” and “He hasn’t put it away.”
3. Replace the Judas-like dread appeal with a clear, grace-anchored invitation to repentance and Christ’s cleansing that arises explicitly from the text.
