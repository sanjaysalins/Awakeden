# Independent review — codex (OK, 52s)

HOOK SCORE: 9/10  
“Judas walked out into the night on feet Jesus had washed” is arresting, concise, text-rooted, and establishes the controlling image immediately.

LANDING SCORE: 6/10  
The closing returns to the washed-feet image, but “ask for what Judas never had” is too cryptic and doctrinally underdefined to land cleanly.

Specific findings:

- “He was ‘troubled in spirit.’” has a serious antecedent problem. On first hearing, “He” naturally refers to Judas, the subject of the preceding sentence. But John 13:21 says that Jesus was troubled in spirit. This momentarily attributes Christ’s experience to Judas.

- “There is a scripture for it” further obscures the logic. Jesus’ troubled spirit is not explained merely by the existence of a prediction. The prophecy—“He that eateth bread with me hath lifted up his heel against me”—concerns the betrayal, not directly the reason or nature of His distress.

- “He knew, and served him anyway” is ambiguous. The preceding grammatical subject is “Some,” and Judas is also prominent. The listener must infer that “He” means Jesus. The intended contrast is important enough to name Jesus explicitly.

- “The sop was how He marked him” is imprecise. Jesus used the sop to identify Judas to the disciple who had asked who the betrayer was; “marked him” may sound as though the act spiritually designated Judas as the betrayer or caused his betrayal.

- “All of them had their feet washed that night. Judas too.” is a defensible inference from John’s sequence, but not an explicit statement that John records. Because it carries the entire hook and landing, the narration should distinguish textual statement from inference.

- “If your feet have been washed too, and nothing more” creates a potentially confusing analogy. Modern listeners have not literally undergone this event, and the script never establishes what contemporary “washed feet” represents—outward religion, proximity to Jesus, Christian service, baptism, or something else.

- “Ask for what Judas never had” leaves the essential gospel claim unnamed. Does it mean regeneration, saving faith, cleansing from sin, union with Christ, or “part with” Christ? The vagueness risks suggesting a second, inward washing obtained simply by asking, without grounding the invitation in Christ’s saving work.

- The ending leans toward anxious self-comparison: perhaps you resemble Judas and possess only an outward washing. There is legitimate room for self-examination, but the piece supplies no explicit grace anchor—Christ’s cross, promise, sufficiency, or welcoming call—to prevent the conviction from functioning mainly through fear of false profession.

- The thread remains mostly coherent—washing, cleanness, betrayal, and Judas’s departure—but the middle briefly shifts from the basin image to the sop as a new central symbol. The sop material needs to serve the washed-but-unclean spine more explicitly.

- The quoted wording is KJV-verbatim. The quotations are sometimes excerpts from longer verses, but I found no altered quoted word. Capitalization outside the biblical wording does, however, contribute to unclear pronoun references.

VERDICT: REVISE
TOP FIXES:
1. Correct the antecedent in “He was ‘troubled in spirit’” by explicitly identifying Jesus, and clarify why the betrayal prophecy is introduced.
2. Replace “ask for what Judas never had” with a precise, grace-grounded invitation naming the inward cleansing or saving relationship intended.
3. Define the present-day meaning of “your feet have been washed” and qualify Judas’s washing as an inference from John’s narrative sequence.
