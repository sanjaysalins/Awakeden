## Independent Adversarial Review — "Ye Are Not All Clean" (John 13)

**KJV verbatim check** — checked every quoted fragment against John 13 (KJV):
- "put into the heart of Judas Iscariot, Simon's son, to betray him" (v.2) ✓
- "riseth from supper, and laid aside his garments; and took a towel, and girded himself" (v.4) ✓
- "began to wash the disciples' feet" (v.5) ✓
- "If I wash thee not, thou hast no part with me" (v.8) ✓
- "he knew who should betray him; therefore said he, Ye are not all clean" (v.11) ✓
- "I have given you an example" (v.15) ✓
- "I speak not of you all" / "He that eateth bread with me hath lifted up his heel against me" (v.18) ✓
- "troubled in spirit" (v.21) ✓
- "And after the sop Satan entered into him" / "That thou doest, do quickly" (v.27) ✓
- "no man at the table knew" / "give something to the poor" (v.28-29) ✓
- "went immediately out: and it was night" (v.30) ✓

No altered words found. This is clean — no doctrinal invention either; "washing but not clean" (Judas) is exactly the tension the text itself sets up (v.10-11), not an imported reading.

**Structural problem — conviction beat is compressed to nothing.** The piece is ~90% narrative retelling (a fairly faithful, well-sequenced walk through vv.2-30) and then jumps straight from narration to CTA in two short sentences: *"The basin was still in the room. He is not finished with it."* That's the entire conviction beat before the landing fires. A listener spends 50+ seconds being told a story and about 3 seconds being personally addressed. It works because the final two lines are good, but it's thin — the piece never really turns to "you" until the very end.

**Landing's last sentence reverts to a fill-in-the-blank CTA shape.** *"'Ye are not all clean' does not have to be true of your heart. Let Him make it clean."* The first sentence is earned — it's built directly from the piece's own KJV phrase ("clean"). But the actual final sentence, *"Let Him make it clean,"* is a generic imperative template ("Let Him ___") stripped of the piece's specific imagery (basin, towel, sop, feet). Apply the test literally: could this sentence end a *different* cleansing story in this same project's corpus — Naaman (2 Kings 5, "clean" is literally the operative word there too), a leper healing, the woman caught in adultery? Yes, easily. That's the stock-closer failure mode the rubric warns about, even though the word "clean" is technically drawn from this text.

**Hook relies on unstated cultural knowledge.** *"Judas walked out into the night on feet Jesus had washed."* Good, non-templated, real irony. But the irony only lands if the listener already knows foot-washing was a humiliating, servant's-task act of love — the piece never states that (nor does it anywhere explain why the washing scene matters culturally). For a "zero Bible knowledge" listener in the first 5 seconds, the emotional charge is assumed, not built.

**Minor:** the piece never anchors setting (Passover, the upper room, the Twelve) — inferable from context, but a true first-time listener is piecing together "who is at this supper" from fragments alone. Not disqualifying, just a clarity tax.

---

**HOOK SCORE: 7/10** — non-templated, real irony, but leans on unstated background knowledge to land in the first seconds rather than being self-contained.

**LANDING SCORE: 7/10** — the penultimate line is earned (piece's own word), but the actual closing sentence ("Let Him make it clean") is a generic imperative shape portable to other cleansing-themed episodes in this series, not fully locked to THIS piece's own basin/towel/sop imagery.

VERDICT: REVISE
TOP FIXES:
1. Rebuild the final sentence around a piece-specific image (the basin, the towel, the sop — something only this text gave you) instead of the generic "Let Him make it clean" imperative, which could close a Naaman or leper-cleansing piece just as easily.
2. Expand the conviction turn beyond two sentences — the piece spends its whole runtime as third-person narrative and only turns to "you" at the very last moment; give the turn more room before the landing fires.
3. Ground the hook's irony explicitly (even in one clause) so a zero-knowledge listener knows why "feet Jesus had washed" carries weight, rather than assuming they already know foot-washing = an act of humble love.
