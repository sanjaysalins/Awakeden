# Independent review — codex (OK, 70s)

**Hook: 7/10.** “The man who carried the cross of Jesus was walking the other way” is arresting, but its central claim exceeds the text. Mark says Simon was “coming out of the country”; it does not explicitly say where he intended to go. The hook therefore depends on a plausible reconstruction presented as fact.

**Landing: 5/10.** “Freely, this time. After Jesus.” echoes Luke, but “After Jesus” could end almost any discipleship piece. Worse, “freely” weakens Hebrews’ actual force: “Let us go forth” is an exhortation to believers to identify with Christ and bear reproach, not merely a voluntary invitation contrasted with Simon’s compulsion.

Specific findings:

- “likely just arriving for Passover” is unsupported speculation. The narrative identifies Simon as a Cyrenian “coming out of the country,” but gives neither his destination nor his reason for traveling. “Wrong road, wrong minute” further invents Simon’s perspective; the text never calls his presence a mistake or inconvenience.

- “They grabbed him, and Simon was turned around” heightens the same inference. “Compel” supports coercion, but “turned around” depends on an unproved route reconstruction.

- “Under the law, the sin offering was burned ‘without the camp’” overgeneralizes. Hebrews speaks specifically of the bodies of those sacrificial animals whose blood was brought into the sanctuary, not every offering classified as a sin offering. “Outside is where the sin offering went” repeats the misleading generalization.

- “You are not compelled. You are invited” changes both audience and tone. Hebrews 13:13 addresses believers with a corporate imperative—“Let us go forth”—and calls them to costly solidarity with Christ. Presenting it as a generic invitation risks confusing conversion, free will, and discipleship.

- “Freely, this time” is not derived from Hebrews and may imply that bearing Christ’s reproach is principally an exercise of autonomous choice. The grace anchor is actually the preceding declaration that Jesus sanctifies his people “with his own blood”; the response should remain downstream of that accomplished work.

- The Simon-to-Hebrews connection is homiletically attractive but not established by either passage. Hebrews connects Jesus outside the gate with sacrificial bodies outside the camp; it does not identify Simon as an illustration of verse 13. The narration should signal analogy rather than present the connection as the text’s own argument.

- “the father of Alexander and Rufus” is quoted accurately but introduced and abandoned. For a first-time listener, two unexplained names add cognitive load without serving the thread.

- The overall spine—outside the city, outside the camp, going outside to Christ—is strong and coherent. The problem is that the opening emphasizes Simon’s supposed reversal, while the middle’s controlling image becomes “outside.” Those threads touch, but the narration does not fully prove their connection.

- All words inside quotation marks match the KJV; I found no altered quoted word. The relevant wording is confirmed in [Mark 15:20–21](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark+15%3A20-21&version=KJV), [Luke 23:26](https://www.biblegateway.com/verse/en/Luke%2023%3A26), and [Hebrews 13:11–13](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Hebrews+13%3A11-13&version=KJV%3BNKJV%3BNIV).

VERDICT: REVISE
TOP FIXES:
1. Remove or clearly qualify the invented claims that Simon was entering Jerusalem for Passover, walking the opposite way, and “turned around.”
2. Represent Hebrews 13 accurately: distinguish the specified sacrifices from all sin offerings and preserve its believer-directed, costly exhortation.
3. Replace the generic “After Jesus” ending with a CTA built unmistakably around this piece’s controlling “outside the camp” image and Christ’s sanctifying blood.
