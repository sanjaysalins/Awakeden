This reads as a genuinely usable device: the small panels could carry a more dramatic/dreamlike "vision" quality while the main scene keeps its grounded diary-sketch feel — a real visual distinction with a story reason behind it, not just decoration.

TEST 3 — character portrait, face visible

Tests 1 and 2 both show Jacob from behind — neither actually showed his face rendered in this style. This one chains BOTH jacob_ref.png (full figure) and jacob_face_ref.png (the close crop) for the first time in a style test, in a medium 3/4 portrait. Face reads consistent with the locked build: sparse young beard (not full), dark curling hair, olive robe over cream tunic, ochre mantle, straight staff, watchful/weary expression. The hatching follows the folds of his face and robe convincingly.

What held up across the style jump (test 1, full woodcut): Jacob's olive-green robe, ochre-brown mantle, dark curling hair, and the straight (no-crook) wooden staff all carried over reasonably well, even though the chained refs were built for a completely different rendering style (flat ink-and-watercolor storyboard vs dense woodcut hatching + cinematic photography).

What's fundamentally different, not just a filter: no panels, no baked title/frame-number, no handwritten captions, no Swirls-of-Life motif — this is a single full-bleed cinematic image, not a storyboard page. Adopting this style for real would mean redesigning the whole page format, not just swapping a style prompt.

Feel: much more dramatic/epic — volumetric light rays, teal/gold cinematic grading, denser hatching — versus the current style's flatter, gentler, more "found production sketchbook" feel.
