# Independent review — codex (OK, 32s)

HOOK SCORE: 8/10

“Jesus healed ten men at once — then counted how many didn't come back. Nine.” is immediate and arresting. But “counted” is slightly gimmicky: Jesus asks, “where are the nine?” The text does not portray Him discovering the number by counting.

LANDING SCORE: 7/10

“Whole still happens at his feet” grows organically from “made thee whole” and the returning man at Jesus’ feet. It is memorable and specific to this piece. However, it lands on a theological distinction the passage does not clearly establish.

Specific findings:

- The central contrast—“The nine got everything they asked for. Cleansed happened on the road. Whole still happens at his feet.”—overstates the Greek. The same verb, *sōzō*, can mean save, heal, or make well according to context. Its appearance in Luke 7:50 does not prove that Luke 17 contrasts mere physical cleansing for nine with spiritual salvation or fuller restoration for one.

- The script risks a works-based implication: the one who returned received “whole” because he came back and gave thanks, while the nine received only cleansing. Yet Jesus says, “thy faith hath made thee whole,” not “thy gratitude hath made thee whole.” If spiritual salvation is intended, the narration must anchor it in faith and grace rather than in the rewarded act of returning.

- “One heard that word — in Luke's Greek, the same words he gave the woman who washed his feet” is imprecise. It is the same Greek sentence, not merely the same “word.” Also, Luke 7 describes the woman washing/wetting Jesus’ feet with tears, wiping them with her hair, kissing them, and anointing them; “the woman who washed his feet” may make an uninformed listener picture a conventional washing.

- “The inspection for a leper already healed. They weren't. Not yet.” is broadly faithful to Leviticus 14 and effectively highlights their obedient departure before visible cleansing. Still, “inspection” should be explained as priestly confirmation of cleansing, not as a medical procedure.

- “Luke saves that for last: a leper, and a foreigner” is rhetorically compressed but misleading. The audience already knows all ten are lepers, so Luke’s final disclosure is specifically that the grateful man was a Samaritan. Moreover, “stranger” reflects exclusion from Israel’s covenant community more precisely than the broad modern category “foreigner.”

- The spine begins with Jesus noticing the missing nine, moves through obedient faith, then lands on a cleansed-versus-whole distinction. Those are related, but the controlling thread shifts. The hook promises an examination of the nine who did not return; the ending instead makes a contested claim about two levels of healing.

- The words inside quotation marks match the KJV wording. No altered quoted word is evident.

VERDICT: REVISE
TOP FIXES:
1. Remove or carefully qualify the unsupported claim that nine received only cleansing while the returning Samaritan received a distinct spiritual “wholeness.”
2. Keep faith and grace causal; do not imply that returning or expressing gratitude earned the greater gift.
3. Choose one spine—preferably recognizing Jesus as the source of mercy—and carry it consistently from the missing nine to the final line.
