A first-time listener may think the text contradicts itself unless the “same in Greek, different in English” point lands instantly.

---

### Doctrinal soundness
**Major concern — the cleansed vs. whole ladder is overstated.**

The closing turn — *"Ten were cleansed. One heard that word… The nine got everything they asked for. Cleansed happened on the road. Whole still happens at his feet."* — builds a two-tier gift the passage does not clearly teach.

- All ten were cleansed **as they went** (Luke 17:14). The Samaritan was among them; he did not skip “cleansed.”
- Jesus attributes wholeness to **faith**, not to returning: *"thy faith hath made thee whole."* The script’s spatial frame (“at his feet”) and thanksgiving sequence can sound like **gratitude earned the deeper gift**.
- Linking Luke 17:19 to Luke 7:50 via Greek *sōzō* is a legitimate textual observation, but *sōzō* is context-dependent (save / heal / make well). Using it to imply nine received only physical relief while one received something categorically “more” (salvation / true wholeness) is **commentary**, not stated Scripture — and it edges toward a contested reading used for freshness.

**Secondary concern — “Then Jesus counted”** (twice, hook + middle): Jesus **asks** *"where are the nine?"* He does not discover the number by counting. Small slip, but in an adversarial read it signals the writer shaping the text to fit a hook rather than following the text.

**“Luke saves that for last: a leper, and a foreigner.”**  
The audience already knows they are lepers. Luke’s delayed reveal is **Samaritan**, not leper. *"foreigner"* also softens KJV *"stranger"* (covenant-outsider force), which is part of the passage’s punch.

---

### Clarity on first hearing
**Several beats assume Bible literacy.**

- *"the inspection for a leper already healed. They weren't. Not yet."* — Clever if you know Leviticus 14; opaque if you don’t. A zero-knowledge listener may not grasp why unhealed men are sent to priests.
- *"Luke saves that for last"* — names an author the listener may not know is the storyteller.
- *"in Luke's Greek, the same words he gave the woman who washed his feet"* — audio-unfriendly meta; also *"washed his feet"* undersells Luke 7 (tears, hair, anointing) and may blur with other “at his feet” scenes.

**Logic tension:** *"The nine got everything they asked for"* vs. the implied missing “whole.” They asked for **mercy**; if “whole” means more than cleansing, the line fights the closing claim unless “everything” is narrowly defined — and it isn’t, on first hearing.

---

### Grace-anchored conviction
No overt fear/gain-loss (“what you lose if…”). Good.

But the **return-to-feet → wholeness** arc risks **works-adjacent framing**: the one who **came back** gets the salvific-sounding word; the nine who **kept going** get only “cleansed.” The text’s causal clause is **faith**, not gratitude-as-merit. As written, the Spirit’s invitation is muffled by a rewarded-behavior silhouette.

---

### Freshness = faithful depth
The KJV/Greek divergence (cleansed vs. saved/made whole) is genuinely text-surprising and orthodox **if qualified**. As delivered, it reads like a slick theological one-liner rather than shown profundity — closer to explaining Luke’s vocabulary than letting the listener feel the gap between road-cleansing and face-to-face mercy.

---

### One thread spine (hook → middle → CTA)
**Thread drift.**

- **Hook:** Jesus healed ten — **nine didn’t come back** (accountability / missing gratitude).
- **Middle:** Samaritan outsider + Jesus’s rhetorical question.
- **Landing:** **Cleansed vs. whole** (lexical/theological tier).

Those are related, but the **controlling question shifts**. The hook promises “where are the nine?”; the payoff is a Greek word-study distinction. The nine disappear as people; only the Samaritan and a vocabulary lesson remain.

---

### HOOK SCORE: **8/10**
*"Jesus healed ten men at once — then counted how many didn't come back. Nine."* — Concrete, immediate, not a template open. Loses points for **“counted”** (not what the text says) and for telegraphing the turn before the scene breathes.

### LANDING SCORE: **6/10**
*"Whole still happens at his feet."* — Memorable, tied to this story’s feet/thanks/whole language. But **“at his feet” is portable** (Luke 7, Mary/Martha, etc.), and the landing rests on the **contested cleansed/whole tier** rather than a crisp, grace-anchored invitation to **Christ** built from *this* passage’s own verb (*mercy*, *faith*, *return to give glory*). Not a stock “come to Jesus,” but not bone-deep enough to clear an 8.

**Both scores not ≥8 on landing → REVISE per your rubric.**

---

VERDICT: REVISE  
TOP FIXES:  
1. Drop or sharply qualify the nine-were-only-“cleansed” / one-was-“whole” tier; anchor any deeper gift in **“thy faith hath made thee whole”** (faith + mercy), not returning as merit.  
2. Hold one spine: either **“where are the nine?”** (gratitude/glory before the Healer) **or** the Greek cleansed/whole turn — not both; if keeping the Greek beat, state plainly that all ten were cleansed on the road and only one came back for the face-to-face word.  
3. Rewrite the landing from **this passage’s verbs** (mercy asked, glory returned, faith named) so the final line cannot sit equally on Luke 7 or generic “at his feet” pieces — and replace **“counted”** / **“foreigner”** with text-faithful **“asked”** / **“stranger.”**
