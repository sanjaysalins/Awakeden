# Independent review — codex (OK, 55s)

**Hook: 8/10.** “Jesus told ten men with leprosy to go — before a single one of them was healed” creates immediate tension without using a template opening. The next two short sentences sharpen the contrast effectively.

**Landing: 5/10.** “Turn back” repeats the passage’s central verb, but it is still portable enough to close a prodigal-son, apostasy, or generic repentance piece. More seriously, the sentence preceding it reaches beyond the text and turns gratitude into an accusatory obligation.

Specific findings:

- **The central interpretive claim is overstated:** “Luke calls it thanks. Jesus calls it faith.” Luke describes the Samaritan “giving him thanks”; he does not call the thanksgiving itself faith. Jesus then says the man’s faith made him whole. The narration collapses two related things into an identity the text does not establish.

- **The CTA invents an ongoing personal accusation:** “Whatever he's already done for you, he's still asking where you are.” In the passage, Jesus asks where the absent nine are. The text does not say that Jesus is perpetually asking every listener where they are. This shifts from exposition to an unsupported divine message addressed directly to the audience.

- **The closing leans toward guilt and obligation:** “Whatever he's already done for you” frames Christ’s mercy as leverage for demanding a response. “He’s still asking where you are” then places the listener among the delinquent nine without warrant. That is pressure-driven conviction rather than a grace-anchored invitation.

- **“Nine kept going” is inference presented as fact.** The passage establishes that they did not return to give glory to God; it does not explicitly tell us that all nine continued onward to the priests. Repeating the claim—“Nine had exactly what they'd asked for, and kept walking”—makes the inference carry too much of the application.

- **The Samaritan detail is quoted but never explained.** A listener with zero Bible knowledge will not understand why “he was a Samaritan” matters or why Jesus calls him “this stranger.” The script surfaces the story’s sharpest social and theological reversal, then leaves it unused.

- **“As their disease required” is imprecise.** Their separation arose from the law governing ceremonial uncleanness and communal exclusion, not from the disease itself “requiring” a particular distance.

- **The obedience-before-healing emphasis risks a works reading.** “They obeyed before they could see any difference” is narratively defensible, but paired with “as they went, they were cleansed,” it can sound as though obedient action earned the healing. The script never explicitly anchors their cleansing in Jesus’ mercy and authority.

- **KJV quotation audit: pass.** Every word placed inside quotation marks matches Luke 17:13–19 in the KJV, including “shew,” “save this stranger,” and “thy faith hath made thee whole.” Capitalization at the beginning of excerpted clauses is editorial, not an altered word. [Luke 17:12–19 KJV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke+17%3A12-19&version=KJV)

- **The thread is mostly coherent but changes at the end.** The piece begins with obedience before visible healing, develops into the contrast between nine and one, then lands on personal spiritual location. That final “where you are” thread was not prepared; the stronger existing spine is mercy received and glory returned.

VERDICT: REVISE
TOP FIXES:
1. Remove the unsupported equation “thanks = faith” and distinguish the Samaritan’s gratitude from Jesus’ declaration about his faith.
2. Replace the accusatory universal claim that Jesus is “still asking where you are” with an application grounded explicitly in received mercy and returned glory.
3. Explain why the grateful man being a Samaritan matters, and stop presenting “the nine kept going” as an explicit fact.
