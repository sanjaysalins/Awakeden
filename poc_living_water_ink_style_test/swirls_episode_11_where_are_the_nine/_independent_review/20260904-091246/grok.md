This is the Sunday-school retell of the ten lepers, with a Greek footnote bolted on, and a thank-you moral at the end. The quotes are mostly clean. The spine is not.

---

**HOOK: 7/10**

**“Jesus sent ten men with leprosy away — before a single one of them was healed.”**

Not a template open. The dash is a real reversal. A stranger might pause.

It still does not clear 8. Word one is church-coded. Anyone who knows the story can predict the rest from the title. **“sent … away”** also briefly sounds like dismissal; the text is **“Go shew yourselves unto the priests”** (Luke 17:14), the Lev 14 path, not a brush-off. The next two lines — **“They were cleansed on the road. Only one of them ever came back.”** — already dump the whole plot. The hook spends its surprise in three sentences.

---

**LANDING: 4/10**  (under 8 → REVISE)

**“Jesus hasn't changed: quick to heal whoever asks, and always glad when someone turns back to say thank you.”**

Portability test: that exact sentence could close any healing-plus-gratitude piece. It is the autocomplete moral of this pericope.

It does not use this piece’s own load-bearing verbs — **cleansed / glory / whole / saved**. It does not invite the listener to Jesus. It describes a nice habit of Jesus and asks for manners. Constitution: land at the gospel, not at application. This lands at **“say thank you.”**

Worse: the middle just put **“Thy faith hath saved thee”** in the listener’s ear, then the close throws that away for a thank-you note.

---

**Doctrinal soundness**

1. **Two-tier smuggle.** **“The same Greek verb Luke had already used — worded differently in English … ‘Thy faith hath saved thee.’”** The Greek is true: Luke 17:19 and Luke 7:50 are both ἡ πίστις σου σέσωκέν σε. σῴζω can mean heal *or* save. The KJV already chose **“made thee whole”** here. Stacking Luke 7 beside it, without saying what you mean, insinuates: nine got skin, one got salvation. That is a contested pulpit inference. Jesus’ own contrast in the quoted verse is not saved-vs-healed. It is **“returned to give glory to God.”** Original-language freshness that smuggles a second doctrine is exactly the banned move.

2. **First-hearing contradiction.** **“Not their faith. Their glory to God.”** Then, in the next breath: **“thy faith hath made thee whole.”** A listener with no commentary hears the script deny faith as the issue, then quote Jesus naming faith. You cannot leave that collision in the air.

3. **Overclaim.** **“never once turned to see who had healed them.”** Luke never says that. They had already cried **“Jesus, Master.”** What Jesus names as missing is return and glory, not recognition.

4. **Over-generalisation.** **“quick to heal whoever asks”** is not this scene’s warrant for a standing rule. This scene: he cleansed these ten. The Gospels are not “heal on request.”

5. **Works-adjacent close.** **“always glad when someone turns back to say thank you”** makes gratitude the product Jesus wants. The Spirit convicts; the script is selling a thank-you.

No invented heresy. No fear/gain-loss landmine. Not a FAIL on doctrine. Still not sound enough to lock.

One altered word: **“and fell down on his face…”** Luke 17:16 begins **“And fell down…”** You lowercased **And** and spliced 15b onto 16 after dropping **“when he saw that he was healed, turned back.”** G1 is punctuation-strict. Split the quotes or keep **And**.

Seven substantial quoted spans. The piece is recitation with glue. That is why it feels like a retell, not one idea.

**Clarity on first hearing**

**“The same Greek verb Luke had already used — worded differently in English”** is a lecture. Constitution bans **“Notice the order…” / “This teaches us…”** This is that sentence. Zero Bible knowledge cannot use it. They just heard two different English lines and were told they are “the same.”

**“a sinful woman who once wept at those same feet”** fuses Luke 7 (Simon’s house) with Luke 17 (the road). Same Person. Not the same scene. Easy to hear as one incident.

**“Nine men are never named again.”** None of the ten were named at all. Empty line.

No **you**. G8’s audience never appears. The nine are described. The listener is not addressed.

**Grace-anchored conviction**

The piercing beat is **“Nine men walked away healed…”** — about them, not the hearer. The ask is **“say thank you.”** That is performance, not invitation. Not a fear close. Still a works-shaped close.

**Freshness / one thread**

Jaded-scroller test: from “ten men with leprosy,” every line is predictable except the Greek aside. The autocomplete take of this story *is* “ten healed, one came back, be grateful.” That is this draft.

Thread swap, in order:

1. Hook: sent before any healing (faith before sight)
2. Middle: missing **glory**, **“Not their faith”**
3. Then Luke 7 / σῴζω = **saved**
4. Close: **thank you**

Four ideas. None runs hook → CTA. The only fresh observation (the shared verb) is abandoned on the last sentence.

VERDICT: REVISE
TOP FIXES:
1. Rebuild the last sentence from this piece’s own verb — whole / glory / turned back — as a grace invitation to Jesus. Kill “say thank you.” If that exact line could end another healing story, it is still stock.
2. Pick one spine (sent-before-healed, or return-to-give-glory, or sozo-as-whole) and run it hook → proof → close. Drop the Luke 7 Greek lecture unless it is the spine and is shown, not explained. Do not insinuate nine-healed / one-saved.
3. Resolve “Not their faith” against “thy faith hath made thee whole.” Stop the overclaim “never once turned to see who had healed them.” Turn the conviction onto the listener without making gratitude a work.
