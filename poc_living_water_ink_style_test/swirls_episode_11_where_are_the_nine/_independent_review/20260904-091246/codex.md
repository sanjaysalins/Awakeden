# Independent review — codex (OK, 34s)

HOOK: 8/10. “Jesus sent ten men with leprosy away — before a single one of them was healed” creates a strong textual tension without using a template opening.

LANDING: 3/10. “Jesus hasn't changed: quick to heal whoever asks” is not biblically defensible as a universal claim. Scripture does not promise immediate physical healing to everyone who asks. The final phrase, “turns back to say thank you,” reduces the episode to generic gratitude and could close many unrelated pieces.

Specific findings:

- “Only one of them ever came back” and “never once turned to see who had healed them” exceed the text. Luke records that only one returned then; it does not reveal what the other nine “ever” did afterward.

- “Not their faith. Their glory to God.” is an unclear and potentially misleading contrast. Jesus explicitly identifies faith in the climactic statement: “thy faith hath made thee whole.” What was visibly absent was their return to glorify God, not necessarily their faith.

- The Greek observation is accurate: “made thee whole” in Luke 17:19 and “saved thee” in Luke 7:50 translate the same Greek verb. But placing it here strongly insinuates that the Samaritan received salvation while the nine received only physical cleansing. That is a debated inference and must not be smuggled in through suggestive juxtaposition.

- “Nine men are never named again. History remembers the Samaritan” changes the motive from grace-filled gratitude to legacy and recognition: turn back so history remembers you. It is also technically weak because none of the ten is personally named.

- “quick to heal whoever asks” turns one historical event into a universal healing maxim. This is the most serious doctrinal problem and could give suffering listeners a false expectation.

- “always glad when someone turns back to say thank you” understates the text. The Samaritan was not merely polite: he glorified God and fell at Jesus’ feet. The stronger textual depth is the connection between giving glory to God and worshiping at Jesus’ feet.

- The thread starts with cleansing while walking in obedient response, moves to gratitude, detours through the sinful woman and historical remembrance, then lands on universal healing. That weakens the single spine.

- The quoted wording is KJV-accurate in substance. The excerpt beginning “and with a loud voice…” changes sentence-initial capitalization from common KJV editions and omits surrounding words, but does not alter the quoted words themselves.

VERDICT: REVISE
TOP FIXES:
1. Remove the false universal claim that Jesus is “quick to heal whoever asks.”
2. Eliminate unsupported absolutes about what the nine “ever” did and the legacy-based “history remembers” framing.
3. Clarify the faith/glory distinction and land on the text’s own image: the Samaritan turning back, glorifying God, and falling at Jesus’ feet.
