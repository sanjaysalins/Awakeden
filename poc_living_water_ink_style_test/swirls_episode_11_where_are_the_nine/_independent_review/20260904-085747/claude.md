## Independent Adversarial Review — "Where Are the Nine" (Luke 17:11-19)

**KJV verbatim check** — I checked every quoted span against Luke 17:13-19 and Luke 7:50. All quotes are word-for-word exact:
- "Jesus, Master, have mercy on us." ✓ (17:13)
- "Go shew yourselves unto the priests" / "And it came to pass, that, as they went, they were cleansed." ✓ (17:14)
- "and with a loud voice glorified God, and fell down on his face at his feet, giving him thanks: and he was a Samaritan." ✓ (17:15-16, only difference is lowercasing "And" at the verse-15/16 join, which is standard practice for a flowing combined quote, not a word alteration)
- "Were there not ten cleansed? but where are the nine? There are not found that returned to give glory to God, save this stranger." ✓ (17:17-18)
- "Arise, go thy way: thy faith hath made thee whole." ✓ (17:19)
- "Thy faith hath saved thee." ✓ (7:50)

No altered words found — clean.

**Original-language claim, verified** — the piece asserts Luke 17:19 and Luke 7:50 use "the same word... in Luke's Greek." I checked: both verses contain the identical Greek clause "ἡ πίστις σου σέσωκέν σε" (hē pistis sou sesōken se) — not just the same root, the *identical phrase*, word-for-word, in both places. The claim is accurate and, if anything, understated. This is a genuine, well-grounded insight, not a stretch.

**Doctrine** — sound. The cleansed/whole distinction (all ten healed physically, only the returning Samaritan called "saved/whole" — sōzō) is a standard, defensible evangelical reading, not invented or contrarian. No fear, gain-loss, or works framing — "still there for anyone who turns back believing" is an open invitation, not a threat, and the operative word is *believing*, not doing.

**Clarity issue** — `"Luke saves that for last — an outsider by birth, the very thing Jesus calls him next."` This sentence is genuinely ambiguous on first hearing: "Jesus calls him" reads naturally as "summons him," not "labels him with the word [stranger]." A zero-context listener has to hold that ambiguity across a paragraph break until the next quote resolves it ("save this stranger"). This is a real first-hearing snag, not a nitpick.

**Hook — score: 7/10.** "Jesus healed ten men at once — then asked how many hadn't come back. Nine." It's not templated ("did you know / imagine / what if") and it does open a real curiosity gap. But it's a numeric tease, not a visceral one — it doesn't yet land the actual shock of the story (ten desperate, diseased men instantly healed, and only one says thank you). It's good, not a scroll-stopper.

**Landing — score: 8/10.** `"The nine got everything they asked for. Cleansing is free for the asking. Whole is still there for anyone who turns back believing, the way he did."` Passes the portability test reasonably well — "whole" is pulled directly from this passage's own KJV word, and "the way he did" anchors to this piece's specific Samaritan-turning-back image, so it can't be lifted whole into an unrelated piece the way "come to Jesus" could. Not a stock closer.

Per the stated rule, a score under 8 on either axis caps the verdict — the hook at 7 puts this at REVISE, even though the piece is otherwise strong (clean KJV, sound doctrine, a real Greek insight, an earned landing).

VERDICT: REVISE
TOP FIXES:
1. Sharpen the hook with real stakes, not just a stat-tease — e.g. lead with the shock of ingratitude ("Jesus healed ten men of a disease that made them outcasts. Only one came back to say thank you.") rather than a bare number reveal.
2. Fix the ambiguous connective "the very thing Jesus calls him next" — reword so it's unmistakably about the *word* Jesus uses, e.g. "an outsider by birth — 'this stranger,' Jesus will call him."
3. Consider one extra beat of clarity on the Luke 7 cross-reference ("a sinful woman") so a first-time listener doesn't have to infer she's a different person from context alone — a half-second bridge phrase would remove any doubt.
