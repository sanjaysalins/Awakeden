# Independent review — codex (OK, 53s)

**Hook: 8/10.** “Waves breaking over the rail. Water in the boat past your knees” creates immediate danger a stranger can understand. The sleeping figure supplies tension. However, “past your knees” is invented detail, and “He” remains unidentified for a newcomer.

**Landing: 5/10.** “Come to the Christ who slept unafraid” uses this scene’s image, but the exact final sentence—“He is asking you — why are you afraid?”—could close many unrelated pieces about fear. It ends on interrogation rather than the assurance the preceding grace language begins to establish.

1. **The central explanation asserts an unrecorded motive.** “He is asleep because He has nothing to fear” presents an interpretation as the Gospel’s explanation. Neither account states why Jesus slept. “Easy for Him, He's God. Exactly” compounds this by making divine immunity the explanation for his sleep, obscuring his real humanity. His authority over the storm is explicit; the psychology assigned to his sleeping is inferred. The proposed freshness rests too heavily on that inference. See [Matthew 8:23–27](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+8%3A23-27&version=KJV).

2. **The narration intensifies the disciples’ conduct beyond the evidence.** “They shake Him awake, raging” adds physical action and a definite emotional state. Mark records their accusation about his care, which supports distress and reproach, but does not establish rage. Matthew records an appeal for rescue. “The very thing that is killing them” also turns threatened death into an accomplished process; nobody dies in this episode. See [Mark 4:38–41](https://www.christianity.com/bible/kjv/mark/4-38-41).

3. **The application assigns guilt to the listener instead of inviting recognition.** “You don't trust the Christ in the boat” and “You wake Him, furious, certain He doesn't care” presume specific unbelief and anger. They also risk making the act of seeking Christ’s help sound blameworthy. The relevant distinction is between appealing for rescue and accusing him of indifference. “He doesn't despise little faith” offers a grace anchor, but the ending returns to an accusation-shaped question. There is no explicit works or gain-loss bargain; the problem is presumptive, shame-driven address.

4. **The script assumes the audience it is supposed to introduce.** “You have admired this scene your whole life” excludes precisely the listener with zero Bible knowledge. Jesus is never plainly named, and the transition from literal water to personal crisis is left implicit.

5. **KJV wording needs a precise distinction.** There are no quotation marks in the artifact. The passage beginning “Why are ye fearful” and ending “a great calm” nevertheless matches the KJV verbatim. The closing “why are you afraid?” is a modern paraphrase presented as Christ’s present address; distinguish it from the biblical quotation. “Silenced it with a word” is idiomatic, but literally imprecise: Mark records a three-word command.

VERDICT: REVISE
TOP FIXES:
1. Remove the asserted explanation for Jesus’ sleep and ground the central claim in his demonstrated authority.
2. Remove invented rage and blanket accusations against the listener; distinguish seeking help from doubting Christ’s care.
3. Identify Jesus for newcomers and rebuild the final invitation around this scene’s specific action and grace.
