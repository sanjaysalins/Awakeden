# Fable drafts (blind rewrite, 2026-11-01)

## jericho

Jericho was a walled city, shut against God's people. God told them to march around it in silence. Six days, one lap a day. From one window in that wall hung a red cord. Rahab lived there. A prostitute, who hid God's spies, and believed. The spies had promised: "bind this line of scarlet thread in the window," and all inside live. On the seventh day, trumpets sounded. "The people shouted with a great shout, that the wall fell down flat." The city fell, but Rahab and her family were saved alive. Hebrews says why: "By faith the harlot Rahab perished not." Not for being good. For believing. Matthew lists Jesus' family line: "Salmon begat Booz of Rachab." Rachab is Rahab. The woman with the red cord became family to Jesus. Christians read that cord as a picture of His blood. Rahab could not hold up a wall. She could only hang a cord, and trust the promise. Trust Jesus like that. His promise holds.

Change note:
- Cut every claim not in the text ("strongest walls in the world", "not one arrow", "one window stood"); kept only what Joshua 2 and 6 say (silence, six days, seventh day, wall fell flat, Rahab and household saved alive).
- All four quotes are now exact contiguous KJV, each with a plain lead-in (the spies' promise is set up as a promise, Hebrews and Matthew are named), and "Rachab is Rahab" removes the name confusion.
- The blood link is framed as a Christian reading ("a picture of His blood"), and the landing is built from the cord itself: she could not hold up a wall, only hang a cord and trust the promise, so trust Jesus like that.

## two_goats

I am Aaron, the first high priest of Israel. Once a year I went alone behind the veil, into God's holy place, carrying blood for the sins of the people. The law gave me two goats, and together they were one sin offering. The first goat I killed, and carried its blood inside the veil. The second goat I did not kill. I laid both hands on its head, and confessed over it all the sins of Israel. Then a man led it into the wilderness, to a land where no one lived, carrying those sins away. One goat paid with its blood. One goat carried the sin away. Why two? Together they made one picture. Long after me, Jesus did what my two goats only pictured. He paid, and He carried. Isaiah the prophet wrote of Him: "the LORD hath laid on him the iniquity of us all." Hebrews says He went in "by his own blood," once for ever, and "sat down." The veil I stood behind "was rent in twain from the top to the bottom" when He died. I was the sign. He is the real thing. The veil is torn, and Jesus is the way in. Walk in.

Change note:
- Named the narrator (Aaron, the first high priest), fixed "two goats for one sin" to two goats making one sin offering, and described the live goat exactly as Leviticus 16:21-22 does (hands on head, confession, led by a man into the wilderness, to a land not inhabited, carrying the sins).
- Removed the invented inner life ("never sure I would walk back out", "years I asked why", "no single creature could hold both halves"); the "why two" beat now simply says the two goats made one picture, which Jesus then fulfilled as both the price and the carrier.
- Isaiah 53:6 is its own attributed sentence; Hebrews 9:12 and 10:12 and Matthew 27:51 are quoted exactly; the stock "Come to Jesus" and the confusing "don't come to me, or a goat, or an altar" are replaced by the piece's own image: the veil is torn, Jesus is the way in, walk in.

## bronze_serpent

I am Moses. In the wilderness, serpents bit my people, and they were dying. They knew why, and came to me: "We have sinned, for we have spoken against the LORD." They asked me to pray that God would take the serpents away, and I prayed. God answered, but not by taking the serpents away. He told me to make a serpent and set it on a pole, so that "every one that is bitten, when he looketh upon it, shall live." So I made a serpent of brass, like the ones that bit them, and put it on a pole. Whoever was bitten looked at it, and lived. Long after me, Jesus said to a man named Nicodemus: "And as Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, even so must the Son of man be lifted up: That whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have eternal life." My serpent of brass was only a shadow. Jesus was lifted up on a cross, and Paul the apostle wrote that He was "made a curse for us." He took our judgment, in our place. Sin bites us all, and the cure is not in you. It was lifted up where you can see it. To look at Jesus is to believe in Him. Look, and live.

Change note:
- Replaced the invented "I begged Him, He would not" with what Numbers 21:7-8 records: the people confessed (quoted), asked Moses to pray, Moses prayed, and the LORD answered with the serpent remedy; "forge" became "made a serpent of brass", and "the very thing killing us" became "like the ones that bit them".
- John 3:14-15 is now quoted whole, in one piece, and plainly marked as Jesus speaking to Nicodemus, so "look" is tied to "whosoever believeth in him"; Galatians 3:13 is quoted exactly as "made a curse for us" instead of half-quoted, and "Roman pole" became "a cross".
- Dropped "costs you nothing but a look" and the "night I never saw" riddle; the landing keeps the piece's own image (lifted up, look, live) and states the link in plain words: to look at Jesus is to believe in Him.
