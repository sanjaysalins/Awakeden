# Independent review — grok (OK, 141s)

Hook 5. Landing 4. Both sit under 8, so this is a revise.

The KJV quote itself is exact: “And the God of peace shall bruise Satan under your feet shortly. The grace of our Lord Jesus Christ be with you. Amen.” The trouble is what the narration does around that sentence.

**The piece argues with itself.** Line 2 says “It isn’t finished yet.” The middle then asks, “If Christ won at the cross, why still future?” and answers, “Because the blow already fell.” A past blow does not explain a future “shall bruise … shortly.” A first-time listener hears three clocks at once: not finished, already fell, “finishing what He won.” The question is left hanging.

**“Under your feet” gets taken back.** The verse puts the bruise under the believers’ feet, with God as the one who bruises. The script then says, “But that head was never yours to crush.” That flattens the verse into “you have no part.” “Your willpower, your vigilance” makes it worse. Vigilance is something Scripture tells believers to keep. Here it is listed as the false way to win. The dash also splits in two directions: willpower as the tool you trust, or willpower as “what’s crushing you.” On one hearing, that sentence cannot be sorted.

**The subject swaps.** The quote is about Satan. “You want to be the one who crushes what’s crushing you” turns Satan into the listener’s personal pressure. “That head” then jumps back to the serpent. One thread does not hold from hook to close.

**The garden image is the poster version, not the text.** “A serpent’s head crushed under a heel” uses “crushed,” then the quote says “bruise.” In the garden sentence the promised child bruises the serpent’s head, and the serpent bruises his heel. “Head crushed under a heel” melts those two wounds into one picture. “A curse” and, later, “the garden’s promise” name the same moment without saying so. “You’ve heard this before” also assumes the listener already knows Eden. A stranger has not.

**“Not force” is the narrator’s add.** “Scripture calls Him the God of peace, not force.” The text’s surprise is that the God of peace is the one who bruises Satan. “Not force” softens that and then disappears. “Never handed to a warrior” is a fight with a claim the passage never makes. “If Christ won at the cross” arrives with no word on what the cross is.

**Grace is aimed at, then left generic.** There is no threat and no bargain, which fits the lens. “You want to be the one…” still diagnoses the listener’s motive and frames the win as relief from “what’s crushing you.” The last line, “Stand where His grace has set your feet,” borrows “feet” and “grace,” then changes the verb to “stand.” That sentence could close a different piece on standing, or on grace, or on feet. This piece’s own verb is bruise. Its own picture is the God of peace, Satan, and “shortly.” None of those survive the close.

VERDICT: REVISE
TOP FIXES:
1. Answer “why still future?” with the cross as the decisive blow and “shortly” as the last bruise still ahead — cut “Because the blow already fell” so it stops fighting “It isn’t finished yet.”
2. Keep one subject, Satan, and one verb, bruise: God bruises him under their feet; say clearly that willpower is not the agent, and drop the slap at vigilance.
3. Rebuild the last sentence from this text’s own picture (God of peace, bruise, feet, shortly), and open on the unfinished bruise itself so a stranger is not told “You’ve heard this before.”
