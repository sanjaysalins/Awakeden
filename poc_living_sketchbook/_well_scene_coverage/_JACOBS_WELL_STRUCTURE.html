Jacob's Well

Long-form and short-form, same conversation, different coverage

The failure mode of everything tested so far: one face, one moment, repeated in different paint. A dialogue scene isn't a face — it's a pattern of shots that changes as the conversation changes. Wide when strangers meet. Tighter as trust builds. A two-shot breaks apart the instant one of them says something true. The camera's distance from these two people is the story of the scene, before either style or line reading matters.

Long-Form Structure

Seven scenes, each with its own coverage pattern — the shots themselves escalate as the conversation gets more dangerous.

Scene 1

The Request

~60–90s
Coverage: wide → wide → medium — strangers, kept at a distance
Wide

Noon. A stone well in open country. Jesus alone, seated on the well's edge, travel-worn. A woman approaches on the path with a waterpot, sees him, hesitates.

Wide, held

"Give me to drink." (John 4:7)

He doesn't rise. Doesn't turn fully. The distance between them stays — this is not yet an intimate scene.

Medium, her

"How is it that thou, being a Jew, askest drink of me, which am a Samaritan woman?" (4:9)

Scene 2

Living Water

~60s
Coverage: medium two-shot, first time they share a frame
2-shot

She sets the waterpot down — the first physical sign she's staying. Camera closes to a real two-shot across the well for the first time.

2-shot

"If thou knewest the gift of God... thou wouldest have asked of him, and he would have given thee living water." (4:10)

Her, medium

"Sir, thou hast nothing to draw with, and the well is deep... Art thou greater than our father Jacob?" (4:11–12)

2-shot

"Whosoever drinketh of this water shall thirst again: but whosoever drinketh of the water that I shall give him shall never thirst." (4:13–14)

Scene 3

The Turn

~45–60s
Coverage: singles only — the two-shot breaks apart
Her, close

"Sir, give me this water, that I thirst not, neither come hither to draw." (4:15)

Him, medium

"Go, call thy husband, and come hither." (4:16)

The question lands like a physical thing. Cut wide for one beat — give the audience room, don't crowd her reaction.

Her, close, guarded

"I have no husband." (4:17)

Him, close — the first true close-up of the scene

"Thou hast well said, I have no husband: for thou hast had five husbands; and he whom thou now hast is not thy husband." (4:17–18)

This is the first shot in the whole scene that has earned a real close-up — not because it's the most beautiful shot, but because it's the moment nothing is hidden anymore.

Scene 4

Prophet, Not Threat

~50s
Coverage: she moves — camera follows, restless
Her, close

"Sir, I perceive that thou art a prophet." (4:19)

She doesn't run. She reaches for the safest large question available — theology, not herself.

Wide, both

"Our fathers worshipped in this mountain; and ye say, that in Jerusalem is the place where men ought to worship." (4:20)

2-shot, calmer now

"...the true worshippers shall worship the Father in spirit and in truth." (4:23–24)

The two-shot returns here, on purpose — this is the calmest the scene gets before the reveal.

Scene 5

I That Speak Unto Thee

~30s
Coverage: one shot, held, no cutting
Her, close

"I know that Messias cometh... when he is come, he will tell us all things." (4:25)

Him, close, held — the scene's true climax

"I that speak unto thee am he." (4:26)

No cut away from this line. Everything in the scene has been building the audience's trust that a cut here would be the wrong instinct — this is the one line that gets to just sit.

Scene 6

She Runs

~30–40s
Coverage: wide, moving — the stillness breaks
Wide

The disciples return, unseen conversation implied. She leaves the waterpot at the well — the one prop that's been in every scene, abandoned in frame.

Wide, her, moving away

"Come, see a man, which told me all things that ever I did: is not this the Christ?" (4:29)

Scene 7

Landing

~20s
Coverage: wide, the town arriving
Wide

"Now we believe, not because of thy saying: for we have heard him ourselves, and know that this is indeed the Christ, the Saviour of the world." (4:42)

CTA lands in THEIR words, not a narrator's. Hold 3.0s per the project's own landing-hold standard.

Short-Form Structure (60s)

Same discipline compressed, not abandoned — the two-shot still breaks apart at the turn, the close-up is still earned, not just cut to because it's a short.

0–5sWide

Noon well, a woman approaches, Jesus seated, worn.

"Give me to drink."

5–12sMedium 2-shot

"...he would have given thee living water." / "Art thou greater than our father Jacob?"

12–20s2-shot → break to singles

"Whosoever drinketh of this water shall thirst again..." / "Sir, give me this water."

20–30sSingles, close

"Go, call thy husband." / "I have no husband." / "...thou hast had five husbands..."

The one true close-up of the short version — same rule as long-form: earned, not default.

30–40sCompressed 2-shot

"I perceive that thou art a prophet." / "...worship the Father in spirit and in truth."

The worship exchange is cut to one line each — the calm beat still exists, just briefly.

40–48sHeld single, no cut

"I know that Messias cometh..." / "I that speak unto thee am he."

48–55sWide, moving

Waterpot left behind, she runs.

"Come, see a man, which told me all things that ever I did: is not this the Christ?"

55–60sWide, landing

"...this is indeed the Christ, the Saviour of the world."

3.0s hold.

What actually got cut going from long to short

Not lines — beats. The worship discussion (scene 4) survives in the short as one exchange instead of three. The turn (scene 3) survives almost intact, because it's the hinge of the whole conversation. Nothing that was earned in coverage got flattened into narration to save time — the short is smaller, not simpler.

The Governing Principles

  • Distance is information. Wide when they're strangers. The two-shot has to be earned by her choosing to stay (setting down the waterpot). It has to break apart the instant the conversation turns dangerous (the husband question). Nobody gets a close-up until the scene has justified one.
  • One shot per scene is allowed to just hold. "I that speak unto thee am he" gets zero cuts. If everything is a close-up, nothing is.
  • A prop can carry the arc. The waterpot: filled with purpose in scene 1, set down in scene 2 (the first sign of trust), abandoned in scene 6 (she doesn't need it anymore). That's staging doing narrative work no line of dialogue has to do.
  • The CTA lands in a character's own mouth, not a narrator's. "This is indeed the Christ, the Saviour of the world" is the town's own verdict, verbatim KJV — nobody breaks the fourth wall to explain it.
  • Short-form compresses beats, not craft. The same shot grammar (wide/two-shot/broken two-shot/earned close-up/wide) survives at 60 seconds. What's cut is repetition inside a beat, never the beat's own shape.

This is what "storytelling" means as distinct from "style" — the coverage pattern above would hold whether it's rendered in oil, ink, or the video-game-cinematic register from the last round. The visual style question is now a separate, smaller decision sitting on top of a structure that already works.
