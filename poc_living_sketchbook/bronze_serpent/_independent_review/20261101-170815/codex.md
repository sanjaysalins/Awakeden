# Independent review — codex (OK, 68s)

**Hook: 8/10.** “My people were dying of snakebite — and God told me to forge a snake” creates immediate danger and a specific, surprising response. “I am Moses” assumes recognition, but the next sentence supplies enough context to hold a stranger.

**Landing: 7/10.** “Look, and live” grows directly from this story’s action; it earns more than the stock-closer cap. But the script never explains what looking to Jesus means for someone hearing about Him for the first time.

1. **“He would not” turns an inference into God’s explicit refusal.** Numbers records the people requesting removal, Moses praying, and God providing the serpent. It does not record God refusing to remove the snakes or establish how long they remained. The categorical claim adds certainty the text does not supply. “I begged Him” similarly intensifies the recorded prayer into an invented emotional detail. [Numbers 21:7–9, KJV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Numbers+21%3A7-9%2CJohn+3%3A14-15&version=KJV)

2. **“We had spoken against God” inaccurately folds Moses into the recorded offense.** The people confess that they spoke against God **and Moses**; Moses intercedes for them. Corporate identification is possible, but first-person narration makes this sound like Moses admitting participation in this particular rebellion. [Numbers 21:7, KJV](https://biblia.com/bible/kjv/numbers/21/6-9)

3. **“I speak now from the far side of my life, by the light that came after” introduces an unsupported speaking position and interrupts the thread.** This is not necessarily a doctrinal assertion about the afterlife, but it makes a dead biblical figure sound like an authoritative eyewitness to later revelation. “A night I never saw” then highlights the device instead of clarifying the story. For a newcomer, “one they called Teacher” and “a seeker” add two unnamed people immediately before the most important connection.

4. **“Costs you nothing but a look” leaves the saving response dangerously vague.** The problem is not that looking necessarily teaches works salvation. It is that a newcomer can hear effortless visual attention as the cure. Jesus connects the lifted serpent to believing in Him; the narration stops its biblical quotation before that explanation. Grace is free, but this wording needs to make trust in Christ intelligible. [John 3:14–15, KJV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Numbers+21%3A7-9%2CJohn+3%3A14-15&version=KJV)

5. **“It hangs in plain sight” blurs the metaphor at the point of invitation.** What presently hangs where the listener can see it? The historical cross, Jesus Himself, or a crucifix? Combined with the undefined “look,” this makes the CTA less clear on first hearing.

**KJV check:** There are no quotation marks in the artifact. The embedded John 3:14 sentence nevertheless matches the KJV verbatim; no altered word found. “Bronze” is narration, so its difference from KJV “brass” is not a quotation violation.

**Doctrinal and grace assessment:** The serpent-to-Christ connection is explicitly biblical, and “made a curse for us” has direct scriptural support. Judgment language here is not automatically fear manipulation; there is no explicit threat or reward bargain. The weakness is explanatory precision, not the presence of judgment. [Galatians 3:13, KJV](https://biblehub.com/kjv/galatians/3-13.htm)

VERDICT: REVISE
TOP FIXES:
1. Remove unsupported certainty about God’s refusal and Moses’ participation in the rebellion.
2. Explain looking as believing in Christ; resolve “costs you nothing but a look” and “hangs in plain sight.”
3. Simplify the posthumous Moses conceit so the serpent-to-cross connection remains the single thread.
