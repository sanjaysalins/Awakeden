## Beat 1 — I was there
I am Boaz of Bethlehem, and once I was the richest man you would meet on the road into town — fields of barley to the hills, and a name that stood among the kindred of Elimelech.

I want to tell you about a morning at the city gate. I rose early and sat down in the dust, and called ten of the elders to be my witnesses. A parcel of land was to be bought back, a poor widow's only hope, and a destitute foreign woman's whole future hung on what one man would say in that gate. I was that man. Before all the people I bought it all, sealed it by the old custom of the shoe, and took a Moabitess to be my wife. I did not know then that I was acting out something far larger than my own hands could hold.

## Beat 2 — The world
Let me tell you the weight of it.

There had been famine, and a man of my own kindred, Elimelech, had taken his wife Naomi and his two sons to the fields of Moab — and there all three men died. Naomi came home empty, bitter to the bone. And she did not come alone. She brought a daughter-in-law, Ruth, a woman of Moab — a people my fathers were taught to keep at arm's length. A foreigner. A widow. Penniless. With no field and no claim the law of Israel was bound to honour.

I first saw her gleaning in my barley field, gathering the scraps the reapers dropped — all the bread a woman like her could earn. When I showed her a little kindness she fell on her face, astonished that anyone would even look at her:

**[Ruth]** **"Why have I found grace in thine eyes, that thou shouldest take knowledge of me, seeing I am a stranger?"**

A stranger — that was the word she used of herself. But I had heard how she had clung to her bitter mother-in-law and left her own gods behind, and I blessed her, and prayed that the God of Israel, **"under whose wings thou art come to trust,"** would repay her. I did not guess that He meant to repay her through me.

## Beat 3 — The act
Now let me tell you what I did with my own hands, for I remember each step.

First I gave her my field, and told her to stay close where I could watch over her: **"Go not to glean in another field, neither go from hence, but abide here fast by my maidens."** And I charged my young men to leave her unharmed — **"have I not charged the young men that they shall not touch thee?"** — for a poor foreign widow had no one to defend her but the man who chose to.

Then, in the night, on the threshing floor, she lay at my feet and asked me for the one thing only a kinsman could give:

**[Ruth]** **"I am Ruth thine handmaid: spread therefore thy skirt over thine handmaid; for thou art a near kinsman."**

She was asking me to redeem her — to spend my own name and wealth to buy back her dead husband's land and lift her out of nothing. I would have done it that hour. But I was honest with her, for a man stood closer in blood than I: **"there is a kinsman nearer than I."** The right was his to refuse before it was mine to take.

So I went up to the gate. And when that nearer kinsman would not pay the price, I stood before the elders and bought everything — the land, the name, the widow. **"Ye are witnesses this day, that I have bought all that was Elimelech's,"** I told them, and then the words I had waited my life to say: **"Moreover Ruth the Moabitess, the wife of Mahlon, have I purchased to be my wife, to raise up the name of the dead upon his inheritance."** And to seal it, by the old custom — **"a man plucked off his shoe, and gave it to his neighbour"** — the nearer kinsman drew off his shoe and gave it to me. It was done. She was mine, and the dead man's name would not be cut off.

## Beat 4 — The strange detail
But here is the thing that never let me rest, the riddle I turned over for the rest of my days.

Redemption was not free — it cost the redeemer everything. The field I bought did not enlarge my estate; the law said it must raise up the dead man's name, not mine. By every cold reckoning a careful man keeps, I spent myself and gained no profit — nothing but her. And she was worth it all.

That is why the nearer kinsman walked away. He could do arithmetic. When he learned that to redeem the land he must also take the widow and pour his money into a name not his own, he said it plainly:

**[the kinsman]** **"I cannot redeem it for myself, lest I mar mine own inheritance: redeem thou my right to thyself; for I cannot redeem it."**

Lest I mar mine own inheritance. He would not spend himself on someone with nothing to give back. And I have asked myself a thousand times why I would. Redemption is costly, and only love will pay it — the price always falls on the redeemer.

## Beat 5 — The wrestling
And I will be honest with you, as an honest man must be: I wrestled before I went up to that gate.

She was a Moabitess. Moab carried a reproach in Israel; the law named that nation and held it at a distance, even to its tenth generation. By the cold letter of it, what claim did a daughter of Moab have on the inheritance of one of our families? And I had a name, a house, an inheritance my fathers had handed down. To bind all of it to a foreign widow and a dead man's debt — the sensible thing was to do exactly what the nearer kinsman did, and let mercy be someone else's risk.

So which was it — was kindness to the outsider a breach of the law, or the very heart the law had been pointing at all along? I could not silence the question. I only knew what I had seen: she had left everything to hide under the wings of the God of Israel — **"how thou hast left thy father and thy mother, and the land of thy nativity, and art come unto a people which thou knewest not heretofore."** A stranger had come home, and I could not turn her away. I did not yet understand why my heart would not count the cost.

## Beat 6 — The reveal
I did not see the whole of it in my own day. I tell you now by the light of the word written long after me — the light that showed me what all my work had always been a shadow of.

There came One out of my own town of Bethlehem — for the child Ruth bore me was Obed, and Scripture says of him, **"he is the father of Jesse, the father of David"** — and out of David's house came a Redeemer greater than I: Jesus, the Christ. He is the true Kinsman-Redeemer, and He did what I only acted out in a barley field.

He had the right, for He took on our flesh and became our near kinsman, a brother to the poor and the foreign and the lost. He had the wealth — not silver, but His own life's blood. And He had the one thing the nearer kinsman never had: He was willing to be marred. The curse that should have fallen on us fell on Him instead, as the Scripture says:

**"Christ hath redeemed us from the curse of the law, being made a curse for us."**

That is the substitution my barley field could never show you. We owed the law a debt we could not pay, and the penalty was a curse; He stepped into the gate in our place and bore it Himself, that we might go free; for Paul wrote:

**"Who gave himself for us, that he might redeem us from all iniquity."**

And then He did the thing I understand best, for I lived it. He did not redeem us and leave us at a distance. He took the redeemed to be His own bride, the way I took Ruth, lifting the destitute outsider out of her poverty and giving her His name forever, as Paul says of Christ and His bride:

**"Christ also loved the church, and gave himself for it"** — **"that he might present it to himself a glorious church, not having spot, or wrinkle, or any such thing."**

A Moabitess in the line of the Messiah — the outsider brought all the way in. I had no idea, in that gate, that I was sketching the gospel.

## Beat 7 — The invitation
So hear me — be still. If you have ever felt like that woman in my field — poor, a stranger with nothing to bring — this is for you.

Do not come to me; I am only the shadow. But weigh redemption's cost. I paid silver and my name for a widow's poverty; Jesus paid His own blood, marred where the nearer kinsman would not be. The curse you could not carry, He bore for you. He leaves you not alone, but gives you His name and brings you home as His bride. Sit with that.

The redeemed never pay. So I ask you what I once asked at that gate, now without fear: will you let the Redeemer pay what you cannot, and be His own? Come to Jesus, the true Kinsman-Redeemer. Be redeemed.
