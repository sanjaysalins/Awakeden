# Independent review — codex (OK, 84s)

**Findings**

1. Major factual error: Boaz did not draw off his own shoe. The artifact says, “I bought it all, drew off my own shoe to seal it” and later, “to seal it I did the old custom.” In Ruth 4:8, the nearer kinsman removes the shoe, not Boaz. This is not a small detail; the shoe is the public sign of the nearer kinsman’s refusal.

2. Several Scripture quotes lack attribution before quoting. Examples: **"Christ hath redeemed us from the curse of the law..."**, **"Who gave himself for us..."**, and **"Christ also loved the church..."**. These should be attributed to Paul / Scripture before the quote under EW-INV-4.

3. The quote **"under whose wings thou art come to trust,"** alters KJV punctuation if strict verbatim is required. KJV ends the clause with a period, not a comma. Same issue with **"Christ hath redeemed us from the curse of the law, being made a curse for us."** where KJV has a colon.

4. “First I gave her my field” is inaccurate. Boaz lets Ruth glean in his field; he does not give her the field. This blurs the legal action later at the gate and will confuse first-hearing listeners.

5. “A parcel of land was to be bought back, a poor widow's only hope, and a destitute foreign woman's whole future hung on what one man would say in that gate. I was that man.” This is misleading. The nearer kinsman is the first decisive man at the gate; Boaz acts after that refusal. The line over-centers Boaz and muddies the legal sequence.

6. “Redemption was not free — it cost the redeemer everything, and bought him nothing” is rhetorically too absolute. Boaz gains Ruth as wife and later Obed; the narrative itself presents blessing, not “nothing.” The gospel analogy can bear cost without flattening the Ruth story.

7. The wrestling is partly strong, but too essay-like: “was kindness to the outsider a breach of the law, or the very heart the law had been pointing at all along?” That sounds like a modern theological narrator, not Boaz thinking in his own world. The stronger objection is concrete: inheritance risk, Moabite stigma, public cost, and whether the law’s exclusion applied to Ruth.

8. The reveal is mostly text-connected, but it leans on announcement language: “I tell you now by the light of the word written long after me” and “I was sketching the gospel.” The type-to-Christ turn is there, but these lines tell the audience what to see instead of letting the gate, price, refusal, curse, and bride do more of the work.

9. Doctrine is mostly sound and substitution is named: “the penalty was a curse; He stepped into the gate in our place and bore it Himself.” But “Trust the Kinsman who would not count the cost” is theologically sloppy. Christ did not fail to count the cost; He knew it and willingly paid it.

10. Voice is inconsistent. Lines like “That is the substitution my barley field could never show you” and “the true Kinsman-Redeemer” sound like a preacher using Boaz as a costume. The first-person frame weakens in Beat 6.

11. First-hearing clarity is uneven. Terms pile up quickly: “Elimelech,” “Moabitess,” “near kinsman,” “inheritance,” “raise up the name of the dead,” “plucked off his shoe.” Some are explained, but not enough for a listener with zero Bible knowledge to track the legal stakes cleanly.

12. The long form sags through repeated foreshadowing: “I did not know then,” “I did not guess,” “I did not yet understand,” “I had no idea.” These all perform the same function and make the piece feel padded.

VERDICT: REVISE
TOP FIXES:
1. Correct the Ruth 4 shoe/refusal sequence and related legal inaccuracies.
2. Attribute every Scripture quote and tighten KJV verbatim handling.
3. Make Boaz’s wrestling and reveal more concrete, less sermon-essay in costume.
