# Independent review — codex (OK, 27s)

Concrete findings:

1. **KJV-strict problem:** **"Your lamb shall be without blemish, a male of the first year."**  
   KJV has the words, but the verse continues with a colon. If punctuation is part of “verbatim,” this is altered. Same issue with **"When I see the blood, I will pass over you..."** versus KJV’s full punctuation and continuation.

2. **KJV capitalization/wording issue:** **"Christ our passover is sacrificed for us."**  
   KJV prints “Christ our passover is sacrificed for us” in 1 Corinthians 5:7, but “passover” is lower-case in standard KJV. If enforcing exactness, capitalization should be checked and standardized.

3. **Potential overclaim:** **“the very feast where every family in the city is killing a lamb.”**  
   This is vivid, but too sweeping. Not every family in Jerusalem personally killed a lamb, and by Jesus’ day Passover slaughter involved temple practice and pilgrims. “Every family in the city” risks historical imprecision.

4. **Doctrinal/typological overreach risk:** **“blood on a frame of wood”** and **“Two uprights, and a beam across the top.”**  
   This pushes the doorframe toward a cross-shape association the text itself does not require. It may feel clever rather than textually grounded, and could read like pattern-hunting.

5. **Possible confusion for zero-Bible listeners:** **“Death is coming to their doors too.”**  
   This is theologically understandable, but a new listener may hear it as “God intended to kill Israelites unless they performed the ritual,” without enough explanation of covenant mercy, obedience of faith, and the appointed substitute.

6. **Substitution phrasing may overstate Exodus mechanics:** **“Someone has to die in that house tonight — either the firstborn, or a substitute.”**  
   Strong gospel framing, but Exodus 12 itself does not explicitly say the lamb dies “instead of” the firstborn in a fully developed penal-substitutionary sense. That theological reading is orthodox, but the narration should distinguish “this is what the pattern reveals in Christ” from “this is explicitly stated in Exodus.”

7. **Weak objection handling:** **“Any one of those could be coincidence. All of them... is not a coincidence.”**  
   This sounds apologetically confident, but not rigorous. A skeptical listener may hear assertion rather than argument, especially because some listed details, like “doorframe of wood,” are doing more interpretive work than the passage can bear.

8. **Landing has strength, but the CTA still becomes generic:** **“whether you will stop trusting your own door, and rest under the blood...”**  
   “Your own door” is not immediately clear. It’s a fresh phrase, but the metaphor muddies at the end. What does “trusting your own door” mean on first hearing: morality, religion, feelings, identity, effort?

9. **Grace-anchor mostly works, but fear remains close by:** **“a frightened family in Egypt simply trusted the blood and stayed under it through the dark.”**  
   This is not manipulative, but it still leans on danger imagery. It is redeemed by the security-outside-you point, but the invitation should keep foregrounding Christ’s sufficiency, not the listener’s peril.

VERDICT: REVISE
TOP FIXES:
1. Remove or soften over-specific typology around the wooden doorframe/cross-shape connection.
2. Tighten all KJV quotations for exact wording, capitalization, punctuation, and ellipses.
3. Clarify the final metaphor: replace “stop trusting your own door” with a plain grace-centered call to rest in Christ’s finished sacrifice.
