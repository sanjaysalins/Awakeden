There is a set of instructions in the book of Exodus so oddly specific that, read closely, they stop sounding like rules for a meal — and start sounding like a description of a man who would not be born for another fourteen hundred years. It is the last night of Israel's slavery in Egypt. Centuries of bondage, and judgment is finally falling — not on Israel, but on the nation that enslaved them. And on this night of all nights, God does not tell His people to fight, or to flee, or to hide. He tells them to kill a lamb. Every household, one lamb. Not a symbol chosen at random — a specific animal, with specific rules, written down before a single firstborn had died. Read them now, and they will sound oddly precise. Almost as if they were describing something — or someone — else. Because death is coming to every house in Egypt. "…I will pass through the land of Egypt this night," God says, "and will smite all the firstborn…" Every home. And here is the part we rush past: God does not say the Israelites are automatically safe. Their address does not save them. Their bloodline does not save them. Death is coming to their doors too. Egypt was the target — but that night no house was spared for its nationality; the only thing that turned death aside was a blood-marked door. Something has to stand between the family and the judgment. Someone has to die in that house tonight — either the firstborn, or a substitute. That is the problem the lamb exists to answer. Now listen to how carefully the lamb is described. "Your lamb shall be without blemish, a male of the first year." Not the leftover of the flock. The flawless one. It is not killed the moment it is chosen. "…In the tenth day of this month they shall take to them every man a lamb…" — and "…ye shall keep it up until the fourteenth day of the same month…" Taken into the home on the tenth, lived with, watched, until the fourteenth — four days. Time enough to be certain there was nothing wrong with it. Inspected, and found faultless. Then, "…the whole assembly of the congregation of Israel shall kill it in the evening." Not a priest off in a temple, and not one household alone — every family across the nation, each slaying its own lamb at the same appointed twilight. And the blood does not go on an altar. It goes on the house. "…they shall take of the blood, and strike it on the two side posts and on the upper door post…" Two uprights, and a beam across the top. A doorway, marked in blood, that a family walks through to be safe. And one rule that makes no practical sense for a meal: "neither shall ye break a bone thereof." You may roast it, eat it, finish it before morning — but you must not break a single bone. Hold that detail. A spotless male lamb, inspected and found faultless, its blood on a frame of wood, not one bone broken. Then God says the words the whole night turns on: "…when I see the blood, I will pass over you, and the plague shall not be upon you to destroy you…" Not "when I see how good you are." Not "when I see your effort." When I see the blood. Now move forward roughly fourteen hundred years, to a hill outside Jerusalem — and notice when it happens. Jesus is crucified at Passover — the very feast for which lambs were being killed all across Jerusalem. He dies. And John, standing there, records something strange. To hasten death the soldiers came to break the men's legs. They broke the legs of the two crucified beside Him. "But when they came to Jesus, and saw that he was dead already, they brake not his legs." And John tells you exactly why it mattered: "For these things were done, that the scripture should be fulfilled, A bone of him shall not be broken." The same rule. Neither shall ye break a bone thereof. A rule about a lamb in Egypt, fulfilled at a cross — by Roman soldiers with no thought of Exodus, only doing their job. And the New Testament does not leave the connection for us to guess. Paul says it plainly: "…Christ our passover is sacrificed for us." Peter calls Him "…a lamb without blemish and without spot." Without blemish. Inspected — not for four days, but across a public life no accusation could be made to stick to; and at the very end even Pilate, the judge who sentenced Him, said he found no fault in Him at all. Faultless, and killed anyway, so that judgment would pass over a guilty house. Now, be fair to the doubt. Someone will say: every ancient culture sacrificed animals. Of course a Christian can find a lamb in the Old Testament and call it Jesus. Isn't this just reading the ending back into the beginning? It is a fair question, so weigh the actual details — strongest first. There is the one bone-rule that has no reason in a meal, landing on a cross centuries later, named by an eyewitness as fulfilled prophecy. There is the timing — the Lamb of God dying at the very feast of the lamb. Then add the rest: a spotless one, inspected and found faultless; blood that spares a family not by their worth but by the blood itself. Even the shape of a blood-marked doorway is only a quiet echo beside these. Any one of them could be coincidence. All of them, woven through one ritual God commanded before the first Passover ever happened, is not. It is a design finally stepping into the light. And here is the heart of it. In Egypt, the lamb did not make the family better. It died instead of the firstborn. A life for a life. The blood said: death has already visited this house — it fell on the lamb, so it will pass over the child. That is what the cross is. Not God overlooking sin, but God providing the substitute Himself. "…Christ our passover is sacrificed for us." In your place. The faultless One, so that the judgment you and I have earned would look at the blood — and pass over. You were not redeemed, Peter says, with silver or gold, "but with the precious blood of Christ." It cost the Lamb everything. It is offered to you for nothing. There is one more thing about that first Passover. The lamb's blood saved no one while it sat unused in the basin. It had to be applied — actually struck on the doorframe. A family could believe the lamb was spotless and agree every rule was kept — and it changed nothing until the blood was on their own door. Knowing about the lamb was never the same as being under its blood. The blood of Christ is not on a doorframe now. It is held out to you. Not earned by your record, not deserved by your effort — received, the way a frightened family in Egypt simply trusted the blood and stayed under it through the dark. Picture that first night from inside the house. The family is not safe because they feel brave, or because their faith is strong, or because they have stopped flinching at the cries in the street. They are safe because of the blood on the door — which, from where they sit, they cannot even see. Their whole security is outside of them. That is the gospel buried in Exodus 12. God never said, "When I see how sure you are," or "When I see you unafraid." He said the one sentence the whole night turned on: when I see the blood, I will pass over. Not your blood — His. So the question that remains is not whether you are good enough to be spared. It is whether you will stop trusting your own door, and rest under the blood of the Lamb who was slain for you.
