Stop 1 · The claim

Healed by looking at a bronze snake.

That is the claim. A desert camp full of dying people, a serpent of brass on a pole, and a promise: whoever looks at it will live. It sounds like a myth your uncle got wrong at a wedding. It is in the Book of Numbers — and fourteen centuries later, Jesus told a teacher of Israel that the strange thing in the sand had been about Him all along.

The receipt — you can check every word

“And the LORD said unto Moses, Make thee a fiery serpent, and set it upon a pole: and it shall come to pass, that every one that is bitten, when he looketh upon it, shall live.”

Numbers 21:8 · KJV

Not when he climbs. Not when he pays. Not even when he prays long enough. When he looks. The whole page you are on exists to answer two questions: did this really happen the way the Short said — and why would God heal dying people with the image of the very thing killing them?

Stop 2 · What happened

A freed people, a bitter road, and the day the ground began to move.

Israel is a few decades out of slavery, walking the long way round the land of Edom. Bread falls from the sky every morning — and they have learned to despise it. Scripture does not flatter its own heroes; it files an honest report:

“and the soul of the people was much discouraged because of the way.”

Numbers 21:4 · KJV

Discouragement curdles into accusation — at Moses, and through Moses, at God:

“Wherefore have ye brought us up out of Egypt to die in the wilderness? for there is no bread, neither is there any water; and our soul loatheth this light bread.”

Numbers 21:5 · the people, to Moses

And the answer comes, not in thunder, but low, in the dust between the tents:

“And the LORD sent fiery serpents among the people, and they bit the people; and much people of Israel died.”

Numbers 21:6 · KJV

“I was there the day the people were dying faster than I could dig the graves.”

Moses — from the film Look and Live

The people come back to Moses with the only words that ever start a rescue — not a complaint this time, but a confession:

“We have sinned, for we have spoken against the LORD, and against thee; pray unto the LORD, that he take away the serpents from us.”

Numbers 21:7 · the people, to Moses

Moses prays. And here the story refuses to behave. God does not take the serpents away. He does not hand down a medicine. He gives the command you already read at the top of this page — forge the image of the killer, lift it high — and Moses, the man who once ground a golden calf to powder, obeys:

“And Moses made a serpent of brass, and put it upon a pole, and it came to pass, that if a serpent had bitten any man, when he beheld the serpent of brass, he lived.”

Numbers 21:9 · KJV

Hold on to the strange detail before you scroll on, because everything hangs on it. God did not tell Moses to lift up a lamb, or a rod, or the tables of the law. He told him to lift up a serpent — the very shape of the curse that was killing them — and to promise life to anyone who simply looked at it. The strong and the weak, the child and the dying elder, all healed the same poor way: they lifted their eyes.

“I obeyed it, and the riddle of it walked home with me every evening. I forged it. I could not have told you what it meant.”

Moses — from the film Look and Live
Stop 3 · Jesus’ own words

Fourteen hundred years later, Jesus reached for exactly this story.

A teacher of Israel named Nicodemus came to Jesus by night with careful questions. Out of the whole Old Testament — the ark, the temple, the kings, the prophets — Jesus reached for one picture to explain what He had come to do:

“And as Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, even so must the Son of man be lifted up: That whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have eternal life.”

John 3:14–15 · Jesus, to Nicodemus in red: His own words

Read that again slowly. This is not a preacher’s clever parallel invented centuries after the fact. It is Jesus Himself, quoting Moses’ serpent as the blueprint of His own cross — as Moses lifted up… even so must the Son of man be lifted up. The Old Testament story and the New Testament claim are one thread, tied by the only Person with the authority to tie it.

And the serpent — the detail that kept Moses awake — finally reads. Why the image of the curse, and not a lamb or a crown? Because on that Roman pole, Scripture says, the sinless Son of God was made the cursed thing in our place:

“Christ hath redeemed us from the curse of the law, being made a curse for us: for it is written, Cursed is every one that hangeth on a tree:”

Galatians 3:13 · KJV

He bore, lifted up in plain sight, the judgment our sin had earned — taken in our place. That is why the healing sign had to wear the killer’s shape. And He said Himself what the lifting-up would do:

“And I, if I be lifted up from the earth, will draw all men unto me.”

John 12:32 · Jesus — red letter

For your own study: Numbers 21:4–9 · Deuteronomy 8:15 · John 3:1–21 · 1 Corinthians 10:9 · 2 Corinthians 5:21 · Galatians 3:13

an honest question —

“If God used the bronze serpent, why does the Bible later have it smashed to pieces?” Fair — and it is not a gotcha we hope you won’t find. It is seven hundred years further down the same Bible:

“He removed the high places, and brake the images, and cut down the groves, and brake in pieces the brasen serpent that Moses had made: for unto those days the children of Israel did burn incense to it: and he called it Nehushtan.”

2 Kings 18:4 · KJV — king Hezekiah

Israel had kept the relic and started burning incense to the metal — and the Bible sides with the king who smashed it. Far from breaking this story, that proves its point: the power was never in the bronze. Metal saves no one. The healing was in God’s word, and in the look of faith that took Him at it — and when people worshipped the signpost instead of following where it pointed, God had the signpost torn down. The serpent was always meant to be looked through, not looked at.

Why show you the hard verse ourselves? Because if we hid it, you would be right not to trust us on the easy ones.

Stop 4 · What it has to do with you

Here is where the old story stops being about them.

“I watched a camp dying. Strong men tried to walk the fire off, and fell; none was healed by his own hands. The venom was in the blood, and the blood could not put it out. That is you. That is me.”

Moses — from the film Look and Live

The Bible’s name for the venom is sin — and it means something more honest than a list of your worst days. It means a poison already in the bloodstream of the world and of every one of us; and, like the camp in Numbers, no remedy anywhere in our own hands. Nobody ever walked off a serpent bite. Nobody yet has out-worked, out-earned or out-improved the thing that is wrong inside us.

And here is the strange mercy at the centre of this story: you were never asked to.

The cure was not in the camp, and it is not in you — and it never had to be. It was lifted up in plain sight, where the strong and the weak, the child and the dying elder could reach it the same poor way: by lifting their eyes. That is not a demand. It is the removal of every demand you thought was coming.

Stop 5 · Look and live

The look is still the cure.

Moses’ serpent was a shadow, and a shadow waits for the body that casts it. The body came. Jesus was lifted up where every dying eye could find Him — and the gospel writes the promise over the whole world:

For God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son, that whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have everlasting life. John 3:16 · KJV

“So I ask what I once asked every man bitten in our wilderness — only now there is no fear left in it: will you lift your eyes and look? Not to the bronze, nor to me — but to Jesus, who was made your curse and carried it clean away.”

Moses — the film’s last words

Look to Him, and live.

Looking is not a ceremony and it is not a transaction. It is faith the size of a turned head: taking God at His word, the way a bitten man took Him at His word in the sand. If you want words for it, these are enough — or use your own; He is not grading grammar:

if you want words —

Lord Jesus — I am bitten, and I cannot cure myself. You were lifted up for me, made my curse, and carried it clean away. I look to You. Amen.

There is no form to fill in here, nothing to join, nothing to buy. A look was always enough — that is the whole point of the story. If you prayed that, or something like it, He heard you. We would love for you to keep reading with us — but that part is between you and Him.
