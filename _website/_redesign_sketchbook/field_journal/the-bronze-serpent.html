The Bronze Serpent

Numbers 21:4–9 tied by a gold thread to John 3:14–16

Israel is dying of snakebite, and the cure God prescribes is a snake on a pole. It looks like nonsense — until Jesus, by night, tells a Pharisee that the pole was a picture of Him.

about seven minutes of slow reading · every plate below is from the film

i.The road that broke them

They were nearly there. After forty years of wilderness, Israel stood close to Canaan — and was sent the long way round. Edom had refused them passage, so the whole nation turned back toward the Red Sea road, away from the promise, to walk around an entire kingdom. Scripture gives the mood of that detour in one line: “and the soul of the people was much discouraged because of the way.”

Discouragement curdled into contempt. They spoke against God, and against Moses, and the old accusation came out again — wherefore have ye brought us up out of Egypt to die in the wilderness? — and then something new. They looked at the manna, the bread that had fallen from heaven every morning for forty years, and said:

the elder leaf · the old page

“…our soul loatheth this light bread.”

Numbers 21:5 · KJV

Loatheth. The miracle had become the complaint. That is the sin under the sin in this chapter: not hunger, but a settled disgust at what God had given.

“because of the way” — not because of an enemy. no battle, no famine. just a long detour. the road itself did what Pharaoh couldn’t. field note: the complaint contradicts itself — “there is no bread, neither is there any water; and our soul loatheth this light bread.” no bread — and we hate the bread. discontent doesn’t need logic.

ii.The serpents

The answer to the murmuring was not a speech. “And the LORD sent fiery serpents among the people, and they bit the people; and much people of Israel died.” The desert they had called a death-trap showed them what it actually held, the moment the hedge came down.

And the people did something they had not done in the whole chapter: they told the truth. “We have sinned, for we have spoken against the LORD, and against thee.” No excuses, no blame. They asked Moses to pray — and the man they had just been speaking against “prayed for the people.”

“fiery” likely describes the burning of the bite, not the look of the snake — the venom felt like fire under the skin. notice what the people ask: take away the serpents. God does not take them away. He gives a cure instead. the danger stays; the remedy arrives.
insert leaf — the wash runs on the day the graves are dug

iii.The strangest cure in the Bible

What Moses is told to do next has puzzled readers for three thousand years.

the elder leaf · the old page

“Make thee a fiery serpent, and set it upon a pole: and it shall come to pass, that every one that is bitten, when he looketh upon it, shall live.”

Numbers 21:8 · KJV

No medicine. No ritual. No payment, no pilgrimage, no proving yourself. The bitten man is not told to fight the serpents, or to climb to the pole, or even to touch it. He is told to look. The verse hangs the whole cure on the weakest act a dying person can still perform — turning the eyes.

And Moses made it of brass, and put it upon a pole, “and it came to pass, that if a serpent had bitten any man, when he beheld the serpent of brass, he lived.” Not might live. Lived. Every time. The cure was absurdly out of proportion to the wound — which is precisely the point being made about grace, fifteen centuries early.

look ≠ climb. the cure asked for no strength. a man too weak to stand could still be healed — if he could still turn his head. centuries later this same bronze snake had to be broken. Hezekiah smashed it because Israel had begun burning incense to it — he called it Nehushtan, “a piece of brass” (2 Kings 18:4). even the God-given picture becomes an idol the moment you stare at the picture instead of the God.

iv.Why a serpent, of all things?

Here is the question that kept the lamp burning in Moses’ tent. If the cure is a picture, why is it a picture of the thing that is killing them? Why not a lamb on the pole, or an angel, or bread? Why must the dying look at the very shape of their death?

The New Testament answers without flinching: because the cure for the curse is the curse itself, borne up in the sight of all. “Christ hath redeemed us from the curse of the law, being made a curse for us: for it is written, Cursed is every one that hangeth on a tree” (Galatians 3:13). The serpent of brass had the form of the serpent and none of its venom — lifted up in the likeness of the killer, itself harmless, so that the stricken could look at their own judgment hanging on a pole and walk away alive.

the journal leaves the left half of plate 44 almost empty. the emptiness is the point — the pole stands in the middle of nothing, and the shadow does the preaching.

v.Fifteen centuries later, at night

A ruler of the Pharisees comes to Jesus by night with careful compliments. Jesus answers with new birth, and Nicodemus — a master of Israel — is lost. So Jesus reaches for a page Nicodemus has known since childhood. Of everything in Moses, He chooses the snake on the pole:

the gold thread — from Numbers 21:8, four pages back
His words · red letter

“And as Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, even so must the Son of man be lifted up: That whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have eternal life.”

John 3:14–15 · KJV

Must. Not may, not will probably. The same necessity that put a brass serpent in the middle of a dying camp put the Son of man on a cross in the middle of a dying world. And the same single, weightless act connects the dying to the cure. In the wilderness it was called looking. On this side of the pole it is called believing. It is the same motion of the same desperate eyes.

Jesus quotes this to a man who could recite Numbers from memory. the shock is not the story — it’s where He puts Himself in it: on the pole. the camp looked at the serpent’s form without its venom. we look at One “who knew no sin” made “to be sin for us” (2 Cor 5:21) — the likeness of the killer, none of the poison.

The last page

Every study in this journal ends the same way. The paper itself gives way, and behind the page there was always the same face.

“For God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son, that whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have everlasting life.” John 3:16 · KJV

If you have read this far, you have already done the hard part of the old cure: you have looked. The serpent’s pole asked nothing else, and the cross asks nothing less — not a climb, not a payment, a look that becomes belief. Look, and live.
