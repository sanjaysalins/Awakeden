**My ranking: Z > X > Y.** Z has the clearest chain of events and the strongest connection between its opening and ending. X has an engaging misunderstanding to resolve. Y builds its central accusation on something Matthew does not establish.

Across all three: the Micah quotation is accurately shortened. The approximate seven-hundred-year date and the statements about Jesus’ death and resurrection are not documented in the supplied passages; those are gaps in this source set, not identified falsehoods.

For **X**:

1. **NEWCOMER:** I can follow who and what: Jesus was born in the promised town, although people associated Him with somewhere else. That makes the birthplace evidence for His identity. But “Galilee” sounds like another town; nothing explains that it is a region. “Shall Christ come out of Galilee?” introduces an unexplained title in old English. “From everlasting” and “He lived before He was born” introduce another difficult idea just as the story ends. “Rose the third day” may not clearly mean returning from death.

2. **BIBLE READER:** The connection between John’s divided crowd and the Bethlehem birth gives familiar material an interesting angle. It offers more than a straightforward Christmas retelling.

3. **CURIOSITY GAP: 8/10.** The opening creates a clear objection: does Jesus come from the wrong place? His birthplace answers it. That payoff happens before the ending, however; the final move into pre-existence feels like a second subject.

4. **ACCURATE + HONEST:**  
   - “Shall Christ come out of Galilee?” is exact KJV.
   - The birth in Bethlehem and tax-related journey are supported.
   - “Jesus grew up in Galilee” is not established by these excerpts.
   - “They did not know where He was born” is a plausible inference, not something John explicitly states.
   - “The crowd knew the promise” broadens what John attributes to speakers within a divided crowd.
   - “He lived before He was born” expresses a Christian interpretation of “from everlasting”; it is not Micah’s explicit explanation.
   
   There is no ethnic blame, though the crowd’s knowledge and ignorance are stated too uniformly.

5. **GRACE:** Yes. Jesus’ saving work gets the final word, without a demand or emotional pressure. The transition into it is somewhat abrupt.

For **Y**:

1. **NEWCOMER:** I eventually understand that religious experts knew the predicted birthplace of Jesus. The opening “They” has no clear identity until later. “They did not go to Him. But He had already come to them” could suggest Jesus physically visited those experts, which this story never shows. The practical emphasis initially seems to be their failure; Jesus’ saving purpose arrives at the end. “Judaea,” “from everlasting,” and “rose the third day” add listening difficulty.

2. **BIBLE READER:** Knowing the answer without responding is an arresting angle, although it is also a familiar sermon theme. Its apparent discovery becomes less convincing once I notice that the passage never establishes their failure to visit.

3. **CURIOSITY GAP: 7/10.** Knowing the location but apparently not looking creates tension. The ending supplies a gracious reversal, but it does not resolve the uncertainty about what those experts actually did.

4. **ACCURATE + HONEST:**  
   - Herod’s question, the experts’ knowledge, and Jesus’ Bethlehem birth are supported.
   - “In Bethlehem of Judaea” is exact KJV.
   - “The Bible never says they went to look” describes an absence; the supplied account indeed records no such visit. The broader claim about the entire Bible cannot be established from these excerpts.
   - “They did not go to Him” turns that absence into a definite event. **This is the largest accuracy problem among the three scripts.**
   - “He had already come to them” works as theological language, but a personal encounter with these experts is not established.
   - The pre-existence statement has the same interpretive status as in X.
   
   There is no explicit ethnic blame. There is, however, an unsupported negative judgment about this group of religious experts.

5. **GRACE:** The reversal toward Jesus taking the initiative is gracious and free of pressure. Its force depends partly on making the experts’ supposed inaction the foil.

For **Z**:

1. **NEWCOMER:** I can follow the journey: an imperial order takes Mary and Joseph to Bethlehem; an earlier message from God named that town; Mary’s baby is Jesus. The significance is that God kept His promise and Jesus saves. Jesus’ name arrives quite late, however. “The city of David” sounds like a possible third location. “Christ the Lord,” “sins,” and “rose the third day” may remain unclear.

2. **BIBLE READER:** This is the most familiar Christmas material. The fresh emphasis is the connection between an emperor’s order and God’s promise. “An emperor gave the order. God kept the promise” gives it a clear interpretive point.

3. **CURIOSITY GAP: 8/10.** “Why that town?” is an accessible question with a definite answer. The final emperor/promise pairing closes the opening particularly well.

4. **ACCURATE + HONEST:**  
   - The Micah and angel quotations are accurate.
   - The decree, Joseph’s departure from Nazareth, his connection to Bethlehem through David, and Mary’s giving birth there are supported.
   - “Every man went to his family’s town” overgeneralizes. Luke says everyone went to “his own city,” then specifically explains Joseph’s journey through his Davidic ancestry. It does not explicitly give that ancestral rule for everyone.
   - “God kept the promise” is the script’s theological conclusion from connecting Micah with Jesus’ birth.
   
   There is no blame directed at a people group.

5. **GRACE:** Yes. The ending connects the fulfilled promise to Jesus’ saving work naturally, without turning it into a demand.

**My #1 is Z.** Its one most-needed fix is to replace:

> “But the Roman emperor ordered a tax, and every man went to his family’s town. Joseph’s was Bethlehem.”

with:

> “But the Roman emperor ordered everyone to be taxed in their own town. Joseph went to Bethlehem because he belonged to King David’s family.”

For a newcomer, the remaining confusion is chiefly **“the city of David” meaning Bethlehem**, **“Christ” as a title**, **what “sins” means**, and **“rose” meaning came back from death**. Naming Jesus only in the final sentence also delays identifying the central person.
