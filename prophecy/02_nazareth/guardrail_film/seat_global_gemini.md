## 1. WHO IS THIS FOR
This is for a young, bilingual commuter in Brazil or the Philippines scrolling Shorts on a phone. The viewer who stops is hooked by the mystery in Line 1 ("stopped reading in the middle of a sentence"). The viewer who finishes wants to see the resolution of the cliffhanger. The viewer who shares is a believer wanting to communicate grace over judgment. The viewer who is NOT served is a non-native English speaker who gets entirely lost in the archaic, untranslatable idioms in Line 6 and Line 8. 

## 2. MAIN TAKEAWAY
Jesus intentionally paused God's judgment to offer us grace, and He chose to die on the cross to keep that offer open. This is carried beautifully by Line 13 ("He stopped reading at the welcome. It is still open, for you, today."). Yes, the visual contrast between escaping the cliff and staying on the cross makes this incredibly clear.

## 3. GLOBAL AUDIENCE
The visual storytelling is universally understood: angry mobs, scrolls, and crosses translate across all cultures. However, the script relies on insider English and archaic phrasing that will break auto-captions and confuse ESL readers. 

In Line 6, Jesus says, "fulfilled in your ears." In many languages, translating this literally sounds like a medical condition or something being physically stuffed into an ear canal. 

In Line 8, Jesus says, "Physician, heal thyself." "Physician" is a rare, high-level vocabulary word for non-native speakers who only know "doctor." "Thyself" is dead English. Furthermore, the cultural idiom (meaning "do your miracles here in your hometown") is completely lost without context. An ESL viewer will wonder why they are suddenly talking about medical doctors.

## 4. BIBLICALLY ACCURATE
* RIGHT: Line 1 ("Jesus stopped reading in the middle of a sentence") accurately reflects the mid-verse pause in Luke 4:20.
* RIGHT: Line 4 ("and the day of vengeance of our God") accurately quotes the second half of Isaiah 61:2.
* RIGHT: Line 9 ("He reminded them God once sent help to foreigners, not to them") accurately summarizes the stories of Elijah and Elisha in Luke 4:25-27.
* RIGHT: Line 11 ("He saved others; let him save himself") is a direct, accurate quote from Luke 23:35.

## 5. BEST VERSION POSSIBLE
The best explainer in the world would not assume the global audience understands 17th-century English idioms. The ONE highest-value change is to add a plain-English narrator gloss right before Line 8 so the viewer understands *why* the town is mad, rather than leaving them confused about a random medical quote. 
Score: 7/10.

## CURIOSITY GAP
Opens at Line 1 ("Jesus stopped reading in the middle of a sentence"); pays off at Line 5 ("He left the day of vengeance unread"). 
Score: 9/10. It is highly visual, immediate, and absolutely worth the 16-second wait.

## NEW INSIGHT
Connecting the hometown rejection to the crucifixion. Most people read the Nazareth cliff story (Line 9) in isolation. By juxtaposing His escape from the cliff ("He walked right through them") with His submission at Calvary in Line 12 ("This time, He stayed"), the film reveals that His death was a choice, not a capture. This is profoundly true to the KJV narrative. 
Score: 9/10.

## THREE SMALLEST FIXES
* Line 6: "fulfilled in your ears" -> "coming true as you hear it"
* Line 8: "Physician, heal thyself" -> "Doctor, heal yourself"
* Line 2: "time of God's welcome" -> "time of God's favor"
