## 1. Freedom’s trumpet sounded on the Day of Atonement

**A. THE INSIGHT** — Israel announced its great year of freedom on the Day of Atonement; Jesus announces freedom and later gives his blood to redeem us.

**B. THE PROOF** — KJV quotations and excerpts:

- **Leviticus 25:9:** “Then shalt thou cause the trumpet of the jubile to sound on the tenth day of the seventh month, in the day of atonement shall ye make the trumpet sound throughout all your land.”
- **Leviticus 25:10:** “proclaim liberty throughout all the land unto all the inhabitants thereof”
- **Isaiah 61:1:** “to proclaim liberty to the captives”
- **Luke 4:19:** “To preach the acceptable year of the Lord.”
- **Hebrews 9:12:** “Neither by the blood of goats and calves, but by his own blood he entered in once into the holy place, having obtained eternal redemption for us.”

The exact bridge is **“proclaim liberty.”** Isaiah’s promised year echoes Jubilee. Leviticus connects its announcement with atonement; Hebrews connects our redemption with Jesus’ blood.

**C. NEW TO WHOM** — Jubilee is familiar in commentaries and some sermons. Its trumpet sounding specifically on the Day of Atonement is probably less familiar to regular churchgoers.

**D. LANDS ON JESUS** — The freedom Jesus announces leads us toward the redemption he secures. His welcome has a cost that he bears himself.

**E. FILM LINE** — “In Israel, the freedom trumpet sounded on the Day of Atonement. Jesus announces freedom—and gives his own blood to redeem us.”

**F. RISKS** — This is a canonical pattern. Luke does not explicitly explain the atonement connection. Do not claim that Jesus spoke during a historically identifiable Jubilee year.

**G. SCORES 0–10:** fresh-to-churchgoer **9**; certain-in-text **9**; simple-to-say-in-60s **9**.

## 2. A psalm already puts these people together

**A. THE INSIGHT** — A psalm brings together prisoners, blind people, strangers, and widows—the same people Jesus brings together in his Nazareth sermon.

**B. THE PROOF** — KJV excerpts:

- **Psalm 146:7:** “The LORD looseth the prisoners:”
- **Psalm 146:8:** “The LORD openeth the eyes of the blind:”
- **Psalm 146:9:** “The LORD preserveth the strangers; he relieveth the fatherless and widow:”
- **Luke 4:18:** “to preach deliverance to the captives, and recovering of sight to the blind”
- **Luke 4:26:** “save unto Sarepta, a city of Sidon, unto a woman that was a widow.”
- **Luke 4:27:** “saving Naaman the Syrian.”

The connection extends beyond freedom and sight. The psalm also names strangers and widows; Jesus immediately discusses a foreign widow and a Syrian.

**C. NEW TO WHOM** — The connections between these biblical descriptions of God’s mercy are familiar in scholarship. Many churchgoers probably have not noticed this particular grouping across the sermon.

**D. LANDS ON JESUS** — Jesus makes the LORD’s mercy visible. The people named in God’s promises become the people his mission embraces, including people beyond Israel.

**E. FILM LINE** — “A psalm says the Lord frees prisoners, opens blind eyes, and helps strangers and widows. In Nazareth, Jesus brings those promises and those people together.”

**F. RISKS** — This is a shared biblical pattern, not proof that Luke directly quotes Psalm 146. Similar works alone do not establish Jesus’ divine identity.

**G. SCORES 0–10:** fresh-to-churchgoer **9**; certain-in-text **9**; simple-to-say-in-60s **9**.

## 3. Behind the foreign leper stands a captive girl

**A. THE INSIGHT** — Jesus promises freedom for captives, then names a foreign leper whose household received help through a captive Israelite girl.

**B. THE PROOF** — KJV quotations and excerpts:

- **Luke 4:18:** “to preach deliverance to the captives”
- **Luke 4:27:** “saving Naaman the Syrian.”
- **2 Kings 5:2:** “And the Syrians had gone out by companies, and had brought away captive out of the land of Israel a little maid; and she waited on Naaman’s wife.”
- **2 Kings 5:3:** “And she said unto her mistress, Would God my lord were with the prophet that is in Samaria! for he would recover him of his leprosy.”
- **Romans 5:10:** “when we were enemies, we were reconciled to God by the death of his Son”

**“Captives”** and **“captive”** connect the announcement with the background of Jesus’ example. The girl directs a Syrian household toward Israel’s prophet and healing.

**C. NEW TO WHOM** — Churchgoers may know the girl’s story. Connecting her captivity with the freedom announcement immediately before Jesus names Naaman is probably much less familiar.

**D. LANDS ON JESUS** — Mercy crosses the boundary between Israel and those who harmed Israel. Romans brings that grace home: Jesus died to reconcile us while we were enemies.

**E. FILM LINE** — “Jesus names Naaman. Behind that name stands a captive Israelite girl who pointed his household toward healing. God’s mercy crosses enemy lines.”

**F. RISKS** — Scripture never records the girl’s release. Jesus does not mention her explicitly; this is the named story’s background, not a claimed hidden explanation.

**G. SCORES 0–10:** fresh-to-churchgoer **9**; certain-in-text **9**; simple-to-say-in-60s **8**.

**YOUR PICK:** **1**—the freedom trumpet’s connection with atonement is surprising, plainly documented, and leads naturally to Jesus’ costly grace.
