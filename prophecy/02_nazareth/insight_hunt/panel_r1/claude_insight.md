# Seat: Bible Insight Hunter

## 1. Jesus named two outsiders, and later in Luke He does the same two miracles himself

**A. The insight.** In Nazareth Jesus named two people God helped: a foreign widow and a foreign leper. A few chapters later, Jesus helps a widow and then a foreign leper in almost the same words.

**B. The proof.**
- Luke 4:26-27: "unto a woman that was a widow" … "saving Naaman the Syrian."
- 1 Kings 17:23 (Elijah raises the widow's son): "and delivered him unto his mother."
- Luke 7:15 (Jesus raises the widow's son at Nain): "And he delivered him to his mother."
- Luke 7:16: "a great prophet is risen up among us."
- 2 Kings 5:15 (Naaman, once healed): "he returned to the man of God … now I know that there is no God in all the earth, but in Israel."
- Luke 17:15-16, 18 (the ten lepers): "one of them, when he saw that he was healed, turned back, and with a loud voice glorified God … and he was a Samaritan … save this stranger."

The link is the same pattern twice, plus the exact phrase "delivered him to his mother." In both stories, the outsider is the one who comes back to give thanks.

**C. New to whom.** Most churchgoers don't know this. Scholars do: the Elijah echo at Nain is standard in commentaries. The Naaman/Luke 17 link is noted, but less often.

**D. Lands on Jesus.** Nazareth got angry when Jesus said grace reaches outsiders. Then Jesus went and did exactly that. His welcome was never just for the home crowd.

**E. Film line.** "Nazareth was angry. He named a foreign widow and a foreign leper. Then, later, Jesus helped a widow, and a foreign leper. Same story. His welcome reaches outsiders."

**F. Risks.** Jesus did not cleanse Naaman, so say "a foreign leper," not "Naaman again." The Luke 17 leper is a Samaritan, not a Syrian. Keep it as a pattern, not a claim that the story is repeated exactly.

**G. Scores.** Fresh 8 · Certain 9 · Simple 7

## 2. Freedom started on the day sins were covered

**A. The insight.** "Proclaim liberty" comes from God's Jubilee law, and the Jubilee trumpet was blown on the Day of Atonement: freedom began on the day sins were covered.

**B. The proof.**
- Leviticus 25:9: "in the day of atonement shall ye make the trumpet sound throughout all your land."
- Leviticus 25:10: "And ye shall hallow the fiftieth year, and proclaim liberty throughout all the land."
- Isaiah 61:1-2: "to proclaim liberty to the captives … To proclaim the acceptable year of the LORD."

The exact shared words are "proclaim liberty," plus a "year" of release.

**C. New to whom.** Some churchgoers know Isaiah 61 links to Jubilee. Very few know the Jubilee began on the Day of Atonement. Scholars know both.

**D. Lands on Jesus.** Jubilee freedom came after the sacrifice for sin (Hebrews 9:12, "by his own blood … obtained eternal redemption for us"). Jesus is the one who announces the freedom and also pays for it.

**E. Film line.** "Moses' law said: on the day sins are covered, blow the trumpet and proclaim liberty. Jesus read those words, 'proclaim liberty.' Then He became the covering."

**F. Risks.** Don't claim Nazareth happened in a real Jubilee year; we don't know that. "Became the covering" needs a gentle, plain image.

**G. Scores.** Fresh 6 · Certain 9 · Simple 7

## 3. "Liberty" in Luke 4 is the same Greek word as "forgiveness"

**A. The insight.** The word Luke uses for "deliverance" and "liberty" is the same word Luke uses everywhere else for "forgiveness of sins."

**B. The proof.** In Luke 4:18 the Greek *aphesis* sits behind both "deliverance to the captives" and "to set at liberty." Luke uses the same word in:
- Luke 1:77: "by the remission of their sins."
- Luke 3:3: "the baptism of repentance for the remission of sins."
- Luke 24:47: "that repentance and remission of sins should be preached in his name among all nations."

Plain-English test: KJV renders one Greek word as "deliverance," "liberty" and "remission." This is undisputed in any concordance.

**C. New to whom.** Almost no churchgoer knows this. It is well known in lexicons and commentaries.

**D. Lands on Jesus.** The first sermon (free the captives) and the last command (forgive sins, to all nations) use the same word. The prison Jesus opens is sin, and He opens it by grace.

**E. Film line.** "In Luke, the word for 'set free' is the same word as 'forgive.' His first sermon and His last command share it. Jesus' freedom means forgiveness."

**F. Risks.** It's Greek, so show it simply ("one word, two meanings") and don't build a sermon on the word alone. "Captives" also had a real-world meaning. Say "includes," not "only means."

**G. Scores.** Fresh 9 · Certain 9 · Simple 6

**My pick: #1.** It comes straight from the KJV English, so no Greek is needed. It is plainly in the text, and it explains the scene's biggest surprise: why the kind words turned into a cliff edge. It also lands on grace for outsiders, which is exactly a worldwide audience.
