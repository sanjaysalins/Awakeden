C1 "ACCEPTED" — NEEDS CARE. Luke 4:19 KJV, "the acceptable year of the Lord," is ἐνιαυτὸν κυρίου δεκτόν. Luke 4:24 KJV, "No prophet is accepted in his own country," is δεκτός. Same adjective. Commentaries on Luke treat the pair as intentional. Two limits: in 4:19 the adjective modifies the year (Isaiah's year of the LORD's favor), so "the year God accepts people" is a paraphrase. The pair exists in Luke's Greek. The synagogue scroll was Hebrew, where Isaiah 61:2 is שנת רצון, and the proverb is not that phrase. A dub will often split "acceptable" and "accepted" into unrelated words. This is a textbook note, not a hidden discovery. Risk: the film sells a Greek wordplay as a pun Jesus made aloud, and redefines the year of favor. Safest line: In Luke's Greek, one word covers the welcome-year he announced and the welcome Nazareth refused him.

C2 "JUBILEE ON ATONEMENT DAY" — REJECT. Isaiah 61:1 and Leviticus 25:10 do share Hebrew דרור, "liberty." Leviticus 25:9 does sound the Jubilee trumpet "in the day of atonement." Luke 4:18 KJV does not say "proclaim liberty." It says "preach deliverance to the captives" and "set at liberty them that are bruised." The second clause is the Isaiah 58:6 line folded into the reading. Luke 4:16 calls the day a Sabbath. Nothing dates it to the tenth day of the seventh month. Risk: a newcomer will think Jesus stood up on the Day of Atonement and that this reading is the cross. That date is not in Luke. The cross link is later typology, not a KJV statement here. Safest line: The Law shouted Jubilee freedom on the Day of Atonement. Luke never says this Nazareth Sabbath was that day.

C3 "HE DID WHAT HE NAMED" — NEEDS CARE. Luke 4:25-27 matches the KJV: the widow of Sarepta in Sidon, and Naaman the Syrian. Luke 7:15, "he delivered him to his mother," and 1 Kings 17:23, "delivered him unto his mother," are the same Greek clause, ἔδωκεν αὐτὸν τῇ μητρὶ αὐτοῦ. That echo is real. The widow of Nain is an Israelite in Galilee. She is not the foreign widow of the sermon. Luke 17:18 KJV, "this stranger," is ἀλλογενής, a Samaritan leper, not Naaman, and not the word "Syrian." The nearer "not in Israel" scene is the centurion in Luke 7:1-10, which this candidate skips. Chapter 17 is not "then." Risk: the film says he answered the mob by doing those two outsider miracles. One exhibit is an Israelite widow. Five passages will not stay honest in 60 seconds. Safest line: Luke later repeats Elijah's clause "delivered him to his mother," and a Samaritan leper returns. The Nain widow is not the foreign widow of Luke 4.

C4 "THE PAUSED WORDS COME BACK" — REJECT. Isaiah 61:2 KJV has "the acceptable year of the LORD, and the day of vengeance of our God." Luke 4:19-20 stops after the acceptable year, and he closes the book. The omission is on the page. It is not a clean mid-sentence pause: the quotation also inserts Isaiah 58:6. Luke 21:22, "these be the days of vengeance," is ἡμέραι ἐκδικήσεως, spoken over Jerusalem surrounded by armies (Luke 21:20-24). Isaiah 61:2 in the Greek Old Testament is ἡμέραν ἀνταποδόσεως, a different word. "Days of vengeance" sits closer to Hosea 9:7 than to the skipped clause. In Isaiah, that day is judgment on Zion's oppressors and is tied to comfort for the mourners. The comfort line is unread too. Judgment already appears in Luke 10-13 and 19. Risk: "he paused vengeance and opened welcome first" teaches a kind Jesus now and a harsh Jesus later, and it leaves the cross out of the judgment question. Sixty seconds cannot fix the word mismatch. Safest line: Luke's reading stops before "the day of vengeance." Luke 21:22 is a different Greek phrase about Jerusalem, not that line switched back on.

C5 "A PREVIEW OF THE CROSS" — NEEDS CARE. Luke 4:23 is a prediction: "Ye will surely say unto me this proverb, Physician, heal thyself," and the rest of the verse is about doing Capernaum's works at home. The crowd does not say the proverb in this scene. Luke 4:29-30 is exact: they thrust him out to throw him off the hill, and he passed through and went his way. Luke 23:35, "He saved others; let him save himself," is a thematic rhyme, not the same sentence. Hebrews 13:12, "suffered without the gate," is Jerusalem and the sacrifice outside the camp. Nazareth is not that gate. Luke 4 names no gate. Risk: the film makes Nazareth a miniature cross and proves it with another city and another book. "Passing through" must stay short of "he could not die." The real grace is that at the cross he stayed. Safest line: They took him out of Nazareth to kill him and he walked free. At the cross he stayed and died.

RANKING: 1) C1, the only claim that is exact and can be one true sentence. 2) C5, the leave-or-stay contrast survives after Hebrews 13:12 and the fake crowd quote are cut. 3) C3, the mother-clause echo is exact and the outsider punchline is not. 4) C4, the omitted clause is real and the return address is wrong. 5) C2, the Jubilee word is real and the Atonement-Day date is not in Luke.

PICK: C1. Put both KJV lines on screen, say the shared word is Greek, and drop "the year God accepts people."

VERDICT
