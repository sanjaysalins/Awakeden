1. NEWCOMER: They could name Jesus and retell the cliff and cross events. They may miss his claim that God’s promise is coming true through him. “This scripture fulfilled in your ears” is difficult English. Isaiah is never identified as a prophet. “Vengeance” may also be unfamiliar. Why helping foreigners angered the crowd, and why Jesus’ death means welcome for the viewer, remain largely unexplained.

2. BIBLE READER: The most distinctive connection is Jesus walking away from the murderous crowd, then remaining at the cross while challenged to save himself. Some churchgoers may never have connected those scenes. Stopping before Isaiah’s vengeance clause is a more familiar sermon observation.

3. CURIOSITY GAP: **7/10.** The opening creates a clear question: why stop there? The missing words provide a reveal. However, “for later” introduces a timetable the script never establishes, and the final invitation does not explain how the cross connects to that stopping point.

4. ACCURATE: The main problem is “He left the day of vengeance for later.” The supplied KJV shows where Jesus stopped; it does not state his reason or assign vengeance to a later occasion. That is an interpretation presented as fact.

   “A time of God’s welcome” is an accessible interpretation of “the acceptable year,” but “It is still open, for you, today” makes a present-day theological application beyond what these extracts explicitly establish. The cliff/cross contrast is also an inference: Luke describes both events without explicitly linking them as escape versus a decision to remain.

5. WEAKEST LINE: “He left the day of vengeance for later, and sat down.”

   Shorter fix: **“He stopped before those words, and sat down.”** This preserves the observation without claiming to know his timetable.

6. OVERALL: **7/10.** There is a meaningful connection, but the script blurs textual observation and interpretation. At roughly 142 spoken words, plus pauses and the three-second ending, clear delivery within 60 seconds will be tight.
