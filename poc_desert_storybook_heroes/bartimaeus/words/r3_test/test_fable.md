# Blind test — seat: Fable

Shared notes (true for all four):
- Newcomer gets the KJV lines only because the narrator glosses them ("Jesus asked him what he wanted", "He trusted Jesus, and Jesus made him well", "At once he could see", "He was free to go"). That works. Only unglossed hard word: "mercy".
- Bible reader: nothing inaccurate in any of the four. KJV quotes are word for word (Mark 10:47, 48, 49a, 51, 52). "Threw off his cloak" = "casting away his garment". "He had said He would be killed there" = Mark 10:32-34. Left out, fine: "Be of good comfort, rise; he calleth thee."
- New for the Bible reader (all four): "stood still" set against the death-march to Jerusalem. That is the fresh thing.

## Candidate W

- Newcomer: Who = a blind beggar, Bartimaeus, and Jesus. What = he shouts, people hush him, Jesus stops, heals him, he follows. Why = Jesus was walking to His death and still stopped for one man. Clear. Confusing: "But the beggar shouted even louder" — the "But" follows "men would kill Him there", so it joins the wrong thought. "He was free to go. He followed Jesus." — no "but", so the choice does not show.
- Bible reader: New = stood still on the way to die. Inaccurate = nothing. "men would kill Him" is fair to 10:33-34.
- Curiosity gap: opens at "Would Jesus even stop?" (line 2) — but the reason He might NOT stop (He is going to die) comes after the question, so the question is thin when asked. Paid at "And Jesus stood still." Second, deeper pay-off in the last line. 7/10.
- Ending: "He was on His way to die for us. And He stopped for one man." Lands, plain words, no "sins" jargon. Two weak spots: it says "He", not "Jesus", so it does not stand alone; it says "stopped", not the KJV "stood still", so the echo is lost. 7.5/10.
- Overall: 7.5/10.

## Candidate X

- Newcomer: Who/what = clear. Why = weak. The death in Jerusalem is not set up at all before the end, so "On His way to the cross" is news in the last second. "the cross" is never explained (no "died on a cross"). The last block is one long 4-sentence paragraph in "would die / would rise" — hard for a second-language ear. "But he shouted" — fine here.
- Bible reader: New = little; the death-road idea only shows up as a tag at the end. Inaccurate = nothing.
- Curiosity gap: opens "Would Jesus even stop?" Paid at "And Jesus stood still." Then nothing is left open; the big meaning arrives un-asked. 5.5/10.
- Ending: good last sentence, but buried at the end of a crowded paragraph, and "the cross" is undefined. Not alone. 5.5/10.
- Overall: 6/10.

## Candidate Y

- Newcomer: Who/what/why = all clear, and the road is set up early. Confusing: "But he shouted even louder" — the line before is about Jesus, so "he" sounds like JESUS shouted. Real stumble. "Up the road to Jerusalem." is a fragment; fine with a picture. "His followers" = good plain word.
- Bible reader: New = stood still on the road to die; "That road climbs to Jerusalem" is a nice true detail (10:32 "going up"). Inaccurate = nothing; "told His followers" matches "took again the twelve".
- Curiosity gap: opens "Would Jesus even stop?" (line 2), then the stakes line deepens it. Still asked before the stakes. Paid at "stood still", paid again at the end. 7.5/10.
- Ending: "On His way to die and rise for us, Jesus stood still for one man." Stands alone, names Jesus, keeps the KJV echo, holds death AND rising in one line, has a pause before it. A bit long; "die and rise for us" is a mouthful. 8.5/10.
- Overall: 8/10.

## Candidate Z

- Newcomer: Who/what/why = clearest of the four. No pronoun traps ("Bartimaeus shouted even louder"). "But he followed" shows the choice. Confusing: "for our sins" — "sins" is not explained here (X and Y gloss it). The stakes sentence is long: "Jesus was walking the road to Jerusalem, where He knew He would be killed."
- Bible reader: New = same fresh angle, best built. Inaccurate = nothing. "He knew He would be killed" is fair to 10:33-34.
- Curiosity gap: best. Stakes FIRST, then the question: "Would He stop for one man?" Now the question has weight. Paid at "And Jesus stood still." Paid again in the last line with the same three words "for one man". Cost: no question in the first ~10 s; the hook is the cry alone. 8.5/10.
- Ending: "On His way to the cross, Jesus stood still for one man." Short, alone, clear, KJV echo. "cross" was set up one line before. The rising sits one line earlier, not in the last line — within the rule, just. 8.5/10.
- Overall: 8.5/10.

## Rank

1. **Z** — only one that asks the question after the stakes, so the gap is real; cleanest pronouns; shortest ending.
2. **Y** — best last line (death + rising in it, pause before it), but "But he shouted" reads as Jesus shouting.
3. **W** — plainest words, but question comes before the stakes and the last line loses "Jesus" and "stood still".
4. **X** — no set-up for the death, ending crammed into one paragraph, "the cross" unexplained.

## Top 3 fixes for the winner (Z)

1. "There Jesus died on a cross for our sins." — a newcomer does not know "sins". Take Y's gloss: "There Jesus died on a cross for our sins, the wrong we have done."
2. "Jesus was walking the road to Jerusalem, where He knew He would be killed. Would He stop for one man?" — too long for a second-language ear. Split it: "Jesus was on the road to Jerusalem. He knew He would be killed there. Would He stop for one man?"
3. "He stopped for one man, and called for him." — this spends "for one man" in the middle, so the last line is the third use, not the pay-off. Make it "He stopped, and called for him." Then "for one man" is asked once and answered once, at the end. Also add Y's "(pause)" before the last line. Optional: borrow Y's wording so the rising is inside the last line — "On His way to die and rise for us, Jesus stood still for one man."
