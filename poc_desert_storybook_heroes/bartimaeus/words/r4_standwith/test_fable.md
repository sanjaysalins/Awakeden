# Blind test: the last line (seat: Fable)

## 1. Newcomer (no Bible, English second language)

- **A ending:** Calm and clear. I see the picture: a man on his way to die stops for one beggar. It feels big and kind. It ends on the story, and I am left to think "would He stop for me?" by myself.
- **B ending:** Warm. It talks to me. I like being spoken to.
- **Does B's last line make sense?** Mostly yes. The words are easy.
- **What I think "stand with you" means:** "He will be on your side" or "He will support you", like "we stand with you" in the news.
- **My confusion:** One line before, "stood still" meant "stopped walking". Now "stand" means "support". Same word, two meanings, back to back. I have to stop and work it out. I may also hear "He will stand still for you" or "He will stand next to you". It is a small word game, and word games are hard in a second language.
- **One more gap:** The film showed Him stop, call, ask, heal. It never showed Him "stand with" anyone. So the last line promises a thing I did not see.

## 2. Full circle? Add or weaken?

- A is already full circle. Its last line returns to "And Jesus stood still" and joins it to the cross and the rising. The circle is closed.
- B's line does not close the circle. It opens a new idea (His presence with me, in the future) in five words, then stops.
- It **weakens** the line before it. A's last line is the earned landing: the story's own verb, the story's own picture. B puts a moral on top of it. The film moves from showing to telling in the last second.
- The echo "stood / stand" is neat on paper, but it changes the meaning of the word. Clever in vocabulary, not in structure.

## 3. Bible reader (KJV)

- **True in itself?** Yes, for some. "The Lord stood with me, and strengthened me" (2 Timothy 4:17) is Paul's own report: an apostle, on trial, for the sake of the preaching. "Lo, I am with you alway" (Matthew 28:20) is said to the disciples He is sending. "I will never leave thee" (Hebrews 13:5) is written to believers.
- **To everyone or to some?** To some. Every text behind this line is spoken to people who already belong to Him. None is a promise to every person who watches a video.
- **Is it grounded in this passage?** No. Mark 10 gives: He stood still, He called, He asked, He healed, and the man followed. "Stand with" is not in Mark 10 or Luke 18. It is borrowed from another book with another sense.
- **In the story itself there is a condition.** Bartimaeus cried out, came, and trusted ("thy faith hath made thee whole"). B's line drops the cry and the faith and keeps only the gift.
- **Concerns:**
  1. Said flat, to any viewer, it reads as an unconditional promise. That is more than Scripture says.
  2. Many will hear "He will fix my trouble / He is on my side in what I want". Paul was "stood with" in a trial, and still died for his faith.
  3. It is not false doctrine. It is over-reach: a believer's comfort offered as a universal fact.

## 4. Series rules: does B's line break any?

| Rule | Break? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| No pressure | **No** | No command, no fear, no gain/loss. It is gentle. |
| No stock closer | **No (close call)** | It is not "come to Jesus / He's waiting / just ask Him". But "And He will ___ you" is the same shape of closer: a you-turn added at the end. |
| No blanket promise | **YES** | "He will stand with you" is said to every viewer with no condition. The Bible makes that promise to His own, not to all. This is the plain definition of a blanket promise. |

## 6. Winner

**A.** It ends on the story's own verb and picture, and it lets the viewer make the last step alone. B's line breaks the blanket-promise rule, changes the meaning of "stand", and softens the best line in the film.

**If the owner still wants a turn to the viewer, ONE better wording of the same idea:**

> He still stops for the one who calls.

- Why: it keeps the film's own action (stop) and the film's own condition (the man called out). It matches "whosoever shall call upon the name of the Lord shall be saved" (Romans 10:13; this verse is not in the pack, so check it against the KJV before use).
- Honest caution: it sits near the "just ask Him" family. It is a statement about Him, not a command to the viewer, but it should be run through the stock-closer gate. My own view is still that A is better with no added line.
