# Blind test: last line (seat: Sonnet)

## 1. Newcomer (no Bible, English 2nd language)

- A ends: "On His way to die and rise again, Jesus stood still for one man." Feels calm and complete. It echoes the earlier "Jesus stood still." I get it: He stopped for one poor man even with big things ahead. A bit long and abstract ("On His way to die and rise again"), but the picture is clear.
- B adds: "And He will stand with you." Short, warm, easy words. I understand "you" = me, the viewer. It turns the story toward me.
- "Stand with you" to me means: be next to me, support me, not leave me alone. I do not know WHEN or HOW. Is it now? After I die? Does He fix my problems? It is vague. "Will" sounds like a promise, but a promise of what? Nothing in the film told me He is alive now and near, apart from "rose to life". A newcomer may hear "stand with" as a vague nice wish, or as "He will take my side in a fight or judgment". Some may feel a small push: now the film talks to me.
- It does not feel scary. It feels kind. But it is slightly unearned: the film showed Him helping a blind man long ago, not "standing with" anyone.

## 2. Full circle? Add or weaken?

- Echo of "stood still" -> "stand with" is a nice rhyme of verbs. Gives a warm last beat, and it is only 6 words.
- But it weakens the line before it. A's last line is a finished statement and a quiet gospel point (He stopped for one man while heading to death). B's line pulls the ending from "what He did" to "what He will do for you", a general promise. The pause-and-land is lost. Also "stood still" (stopped, paid attention) and "stand with" (accompany) are different ideas, so the circle is only in sound, not in meaning.
- Mild add in warmth, mild loss in precision.

## 3. Bible reader

- Grounded echoes: 2 Tim 4:17 ("the Lord stood with me", said by Paul about himself), Matt 28:20 ("I am with you alway", said to disciples), Heb 13:5 (to believers, quoting the OT). Isaiah 35 / Luke 19:10 support the seeking and saving.
- Problem: every "with you" promise in Scripture is addressed to His people (disciples, believers, Paul), not made to any viewer without condition. "He will stand with you" said to everyone is a blanket promise. It does not say how: stand with you in what? Trouble? Judgment? Also no condition (faith, coming to Him). Scripture does say He seeks the lost (Luke 19:10), so a gentle "He stands with the one who calls" is sound; a flat "He will stand with you" to all viewers overreaches a little and sounds like a pledge of protection or comfort in all circumstances.
- Not heretical, a common Christian idiom. Concern is moderate: unconditional and unspecified.

## 4. Series rules

- No pressure: B has none. No (clean).
- Stock closer ("come to Jesus", "He's waiting", "just ask Him"): not those words. But "He will stand with you" is the same family of generic comfort closer. Borderline. I say No, but it is close in spirit to "He's waiting".
- Blanket promise: YES, arguably. "He will stand with you" is an unqualified future promise to every viewer. That is the one rule B likely breaks.

## 5. Scores

- Ending: A 8, B 6.
- Overall: A 8, B 7.

## 6. Winner

A. It ends on a clear, earned, story-based line and makes no promise the film did not show. B's rhyme is nice but the promise is blanket and vague.

If the idea is kept, one better wording that is plain and not a blanket promise, tied to the film's own act: "He stops for the one who cries out to Him." (Keeps "stood still" meaning, matches the Bartimaeus shout, true to Luke 19:10 and the story, no future pledge.)
