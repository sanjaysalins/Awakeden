## Independent adversarial review — *With His Stripes* (Isaiah 53:5)

### Doctrinal soundness
**Mostly sound; two precision slips.**

- **Therapeutic drift via omission:** The Peter quote cuts out `"that we, being dead to sins, should live unto righteousness"` and the piece never recovers that frame. What remains is `"by whose stripes ye were healed"` plus narrator lines like `"The healing isn't something you produce"` and `"The wound that keeps reopening, He has already closed."` That stack can read as **emotional/psychological closure**, not **atonement for sin before God**. Peter’s own logic is sin → cross → dead to sin → live unto righteousness; the script keeps the “healed” half and drops the holiness half.

- **Overpromise on subjective experience:** `"The wound that keeps reopening, He has already closed"` collapses **objective finished atonement** (true) with **felt relief from guilt** (not guaranteed on first hearing). A listener still under conviction could hear this as “you shouldn’t still feel guilty,” which is not what Isaiah/Peter teach.

- **Clever but slightly overread:** `"Isaiah wrote 'we are healed.' Peter writes 'ye were healed' — and points the promise straight at the one who's listening: at you."` Peter is writing to **believers** about an **accomplished** work (`ye **were** healed`), not inventing a fresh second-person promise by pronoun swap alone. The move is homiletically usable, but it is **contested as exegesis** if sold as “watch what Peter does” — it sounds like a linguistic trick more than plain gospel.

- **Thread leakage:** `"it was finished at the cross"` imports **John 19:30** language that neither quoted verse uses. Not heresy, but it ** swaps imagery** (finished-work) into a stripes/healed piece without earning it from the cited text.

No contrarian readings, no invented doctrine — but the Peter omission and the “already closed” line need tightening.

### Clarity (first hearing, zero Bible knowledge)
**Followable, but the middle asks a lot.**

- Strong: `"Stripes — the welts of a flogging"` glosses an archaic word well.
- Weak: `"Seven hundred years before the cross"` assumes **cross** literacy before the text explains anything.
- Weak: `"And watch what Peter does with it"` + the **we/ye** comparison is a **meta-textual move** for a ~60s short. A stranger must track: my guilt → ancient prophet → flogging image → another Bible writer → tense shift → finished at cross → CTA. The pronoun beat is the likeliest drop-off.
- **Sin is never named in narrator voice.** Hook uses `"guilt"`; quotes use `transgressions` / `iniquities`. A zero-Bible listener may hear **therapy for shame**, not **guilt before God**, because `"sin"` never lands in plain speech.

### Freshness = faithful depth
**One fresh move buried under familiar packaging.**

- **Fresh (text-faithful):** Peter re-using Isaiah’s **stripes** language on Christ; the **we → ye** observation, if softened, could surprise about the *text*.
- **Familiar:** `"You carry a wound nothing has been able to close"` is standard pastoral short-form idiom — not wrong, not novel.
- **Missed depth:** The piece never lingers on **whose stripes** (Isaiah’s suffering servant → Christ) or **what “healed” means in context** (transgressions/iniquities), so the freshest material is the Peter citation, not the narrator’s lines.
