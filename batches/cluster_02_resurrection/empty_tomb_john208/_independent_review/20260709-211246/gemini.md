# Independent review — gemini (OK, 46s)

Here is the independent, adversarial review of the narration artifact.

**Doctrinal soundness**: PASS. The script faithfully interprets John 20:8 (John believing the resurrection based on the evidence of the grave clothes, before seeing Christ) and connects it accurately to Jesus' later words to Thomas. It avoids invented doctrine and stays grounded in the text.

**KJV verbatim**: PASS. 
- "They have taken away the Lord out of the sepulchre, and we know not where they have laid him." (Matches John 20:2)
- "And the napkin, that was about his head, not lying with the linen clothes, but wrapped together in a place by itself." (Matches John 20:7)
- "and he saw, and believed." (Matches John 20:8)
- "blessed are they that have not seen, and yet have believed." (Matches John 20:29)
All quotes are exact.

**Clarity on first hearing**: PASS. The progression from the empty tomb -> the specific evidence of the napkin -> John's belief -> the application to the listener is logical and easy to follow for a non-believer. 

**Grace-anchored conviction**: PASS. "Jesus does not ask you to see — he asks you to believe." This is a pure, grace-based invitation. There is no fear-mongering or works-based pressure here.

**Freshness = faithful depth**: PASS. Highlighting the folded napkin as forensic proof against a grave robbery, and emphasizing that John believed *before* ever seeing an angel or the risen Christ, is a sharp, textual insight that feels fresh.

**One thread spine**: PASS. The theme of "believing the evidence without seeing the body" runs cleanly from the hook, through John's realization, straight into the CTA. No thread-swapping.

**SCORE THE HOOK 1-10**: 7. 
*Critique*: "The tomb of Jesus stood open — and nothing was stolen." This kills the mystery instantly. Mary thought the body was stolen. The audience should feel that initial panic of a grave robbery first, so the reveal of the neatly folded napkin actually lands as a twist. By giving away "nothing was stolen" in the very first second, you flatten the narrative arc. 

**SCORE THE LANDING 1-10**: 8. 
*Critique*: "Jesus does not ask you to see — he asks you to believe. Already, he calls that faith blessed." This is a strong, custom closer that relies entirely on the piece's own spine (not a stock "come to Jesus"). However, the final sentence ("Already, he calls that faith blessed") fizzles out. It acts as a passive theological footnote rather than a definitive, active landing in the listener's chest. The real punch is the sentence before it.

VERDICT: REVISE
TOP FIXES:
1. Rewrite the hook to preserve the mystery. Lean into the assumption of a crime scene/grave robbery first, so the folded napkin serves as the actual revelation.
2. Sharpen the final sentence of the landing. End on an active, resonant note rather than fading out on a passive statement.
3. Condense the narrator's transition: "The first day of the week, when it was yet dark, Mary Magdalene saw the stone taken away, and ran." This is too wordy and clunky for the opening seconds; tighten the pacing.
