# Independent review — codex (OK, 190s)

Hook score: 3/10. The opening is a table label, `The pattern (OT to NT rows)`, followed by `Jonah, about 780 BC`. That is not a narration hook; it gives information before tension.

Landing score: 7/10. `Come up out of the deep, and live` is tied to the piece’s image, so it is not a stock closer. But the piece starts with sign/resurrection, wanders through storm/psalm/Nineveh, then lands on running/deep. The sentence has force, but the thread is not fully earned.

Findings:

- KJV failure: `[Jonah 2:3] "All thy billows and thy waves passed over me. Compare Psalm 42:7: all thy waves and thy billows are gone over me."` puts non-Scripture text inside quotation marks and combines two verses under one citation. Jonah 2:3 and Psalm 42:7 are separate KJV texts. Sources checked: Jonah 2:3 and Psalm 42:7. ([biblehub.com](https://biblehub.com/kjv/jonah/2-3.htm)) ([biblehub.com](https://biblehub.com/kjv/psalms/42-7.htm))

- Overclaim: `Jesus refuses every sign except one, a resurrection.` In Matthew 12:39, Jesus refuses the demanded sign to that generation, not “every sign” absolutely. The Gospels present Jesus doing many signs. Tighten this to the demanded proof-sign. ([biblehub.com](https://biblehub.com/kjv/matthew/12-39.htm))

- Doctrinal/clarity problem: `A psalm sung inside a grave` and `A man singing Scripture from inside his own tomb` make Jonah sound dead or resurrected. Jonah is alive in the fish; the typology points to Christ, but the narration should not blur type and fulfillment.

- Chronology/clarity problem: `another Man went into the grave with a psalm on his lips` is muddy. Jesus had psalm language on His lips at the cross, not in the grave. A first-time listener may not know what event you mean.

- Agency problem: `The tomb gives him back.` Acts 2:24 says God raised Him and loosed death’s pains. Do not make the tomb the actor when resurrection agency belongs to God. ([biblehub.com](https://biblehub.com/kjv/acts/2-24.htm))

- Grace framing risk: `fix this diagnosis, this marriage, this bank balance, and then I'll listen` risks accusing suffering people of transactional unbelief. Convict the demand for God-on-demand proof, not the pain itself.

- Thread-swapping: the piece begins with `sign`, moves to Psalm echoes, then storm typology, then Nineveh repentance, then ends with `running` and `deep`. Those are all Jonah-adjacent, but not one spine. Pick either “sign demanded / resurrection given” or “running downward / mercy deeper.”

- Clever-but-confusing phrasing: `the experts stood in front of the greater One and demanded paperwork` is cute, but “paperwork” is anachronistic and weaker than the actual biblical issue: they demanded a sign while rejecting the One before them.

- Typology overstatement: `Offers himself to the sea to save the men on the ship` makes Jonah sound more Christlike than the text supports. Jonah identifies his guilt and tells them to cast him overboard; the sailors are the ones trying to avoid it. Keep the contrast sharper: guilty Jonah is thrown down; innocent Christ lays Himself down. ([biblehub.com](https://biblehub.com/kjv/jonah/1-12.htm))

VERDICT: REVISE

1. Fix all quoted Scripture so each quote is a clean, exact KJV excerpt, especially the Jonah 2:3 / Psalm 42:7 line.
2. Choose one spine: sign-demand leading to resurrection, or running downward leading to mercy; do not switch at the application.
3. Remove overclaims and clever phrasing that blur the text, especially "Jesus refuses every sign except one," "inside a grave," and "the tomb gives him back."
