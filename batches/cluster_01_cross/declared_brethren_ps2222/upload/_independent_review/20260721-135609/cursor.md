### Hooky but honest / overclaim

**YouTube (Short) + TikTok + Instagram — “Most people stop reading…” / “Everyone stops reading…”**  
This is an unverified audience claim, not a text claim. You cannot know what “most” or “everyone” does. It matches the video’s opening line, so it is not bait-and-switch — but it is still rhetorical inflation. Softer: “It is easy to stop at the forsaken cry” or “Many readers stop at the cross.”

**YouTube (Long-form) — “And Hebrews adds the word that changes everything”**  
Marketing hype, not exegesis. Hebrews adds *brethren* language in context; calling it “the word that changes everything” sensationalises without naming the word.

**Facebook (title) — “The Psalm of the Cross Ends in a Family Song”**  
Over-compresses Psalm 22. The psalm’s close runs through congregation, nations, and future generations (vv. 27–31), not only “family.” The short focuses on v22/Heb 2:11, but the title implies the whole psalm resolves neatly into “family song.” Narrower than the text.

**Cross-platform title tension**  
YouTube Short: **“Psalm 22 Does Not End at the Cross.”**  
Facebook: **“…Ends in a Family Song.”**  
Same piece, opposite rhetorical poles (“does not end” vs “ends in”). Not false, but muddy for a cluster upload day.

**Instagram — “Hebrews names the singer: the risen Jesus”**  
Hebrews attributes the quoted speech to Christ; it does not “name the singer” as a separate act. The in-engine red-team flagged this and then waved it through — I would not. Use: “Hebrews shows the speaker is the risen Jesus.”

**“The same voice” (YouTube Short/FB/Long, TikTok, Instagram)**  
Standard messianic reading and NT-warranted here via Hebrews 2:12. Not overclaim *given the video’s argument*, but it is interpretive, not bare-psalm fact. Acceptable because Hebrews is cited — do not tighten into “David and Jesus are literally one voice throughout.”
