## Doctrinal soundness

**1. Bedtime-prayer claim stated as fact, not tradition**

> `[narrator] Hebrew children learned a bedtime prayer. Words from Psalm 31, prayed before sleep, night after night.`

The header note says *“Evening-prayer tradition stated modestly”* — the narration does **not** state it modestly. It asserts a specific Jewish liturgical practice as universal childhood routine. That link is preached often, but it is **not** plainly established in Scripture and is debated in historical/liturgical sources. For an engine that forbids invented or clickbait freshness, this is the biggest doctrinal-historical risk: the **hook depends on a claim the text never proves**.

**2. Death-as-sleep widened without a boundary**

> `[narrator] So dying and sleeping can be the same thing now.`

Paul uses sleep for the dead **in Christ** (e.g. 1 Thess. 4:13–14). Here the line sounds **universal and immediate** — any listener’s death is now “the same thing” as sleep. That is fine as invitation *if* the piece has already grounded trust in Christ/Father; it mostly has, but this sentence still overstates for a zero-context listener and could sound like death is trivialized, not **secured**.

**3. “Forsaken cry” assumes crucifixion literacy**

> `[narrator] The forsaken cry has fallen silent.`

Doctrinally fine for those who know Psalm 22 / Matt. 27:46. For the stated audience (**zero Bible knowledge**), this is a **dangling reference** — they do not know what cry, from whom, or why it matters to the bedtime thread.

**4. Otherwise orthodox**

- “Father. Not judge. Not stranger. Father.” — sound, Christological, no contrarian reading.
- Resurrection as “morning” after “bedtime” — orthodox, not works-based.
- No fear, no gain/loss framing.
