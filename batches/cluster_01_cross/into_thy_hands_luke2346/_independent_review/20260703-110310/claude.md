## Review — "Father, Into Thy Hands" (Luke 23:46), panel-r1 revision

**KJV check — clean.**
- "Into thine hand I commit my spirit: thou hast redeemed me, O LORD God of truth." = exact KJV Psalm 31:5, including singular "thine hand". ✓
- "Father, into thy hands I commend my spirit." = exact Luke 23:46b, correctly stopped at the KJV colon. ✓
- "thou hast redeemed me" re-quoted in the narrator frame is verbatim. ✓

**Finding 1 (main) — the hedge is only half done. The CHILD claim is still stated as flat fact, and it's the hook.**
The header promises "Evening-prayer tradition HEDGED" — but only the *evening-prayer* half got the hedge. Look at what a listener actually hears:
- Line 1: *"And they were a child's prayer."* — flat fact, **before** any hedge.
- Line 2: *"Children learned it for the dark."* — flat fact again.
- Line 4: *"He dies praying **the** evening prayer."* — the definite article asserts the tradition was already standard in Jesus' day.

The attestation (Talmud, Berakhot 5a) is centuries after the cross, it concerns **adults** reciting verse 5 at bedtime, and the "Jewish children learned it nightly" version is a homiletic embellishment (Barclay-family). So the specific unverifiable part — *children* — is exactly the part carrying the hook, unhedged, twice. This is the same "witness over-claim" failure class the corpus re-audit flagged. Surgical fix, don't kill the hook: e.g. line 1 → *"And they were a bedtime prayer."*; line 2 → *"...an evening prayer — words a child could learn for the dark."*; line 4 → *"He dies praying those evening words."*

**Finding 2 (minor scope) — "Jewish tradition made Psalm 31 an evening prayer."** The tradition attaches to **verse 5**, not the whole Psalm. Say "made these words an evening prayer" — also reads better aloud.

**Finding 3 (minor accuracy) — "Three days later, morning came."** Luke's own wording is "the third day" (Luke 24:46). Friday→Sunday is *on the third day*; "three days later" pedantically lands on Monday. *"On the third day, morning came."* is more accurate and just as punchy.

**Finding 4 (minor clarity) — "On that cross, he was paying for it."** The "it" reaches back to "redeemed" one clause earlier; on first hearing "paying for it" can float. One word anchors it: *"he was paying for it — buying it back."* or "paying the price of it." Optional.

**What the r1 fixes got right (verified):**
- "He **begins with** a word the Psalm does not have. Father." — the commit/commend objection is defused. ✓
- No soul-sleep equation: sleep is now a simile ("dies **like** a child falling asleep"), and the glib "bedtime with a wake-up" is gone. ✓
- Universalism closed: "everyone who **is his**." ✓
- Darkness sequence matches Luke 23:44-46 (darkness till the ninth hour, then the cry). ✓
- Grace-anchored throughout — no fear, no gain/loss. "Not because death is small. Because the hands are strong." is the best line in the piece. ✓

**HOOK: 8/10.** "A dying man's last words. And they were a child's prayer." — genuine stop-scroll, no template. But its strength currently rests on the unhedged child claim (Finding 1); fix the hedge without flattening it.

**LANDING: 9/10.** "Commend your spirit to the Father, tonight and every night. He keeps the morning." — built from this piece's own KJV verb ("commend") and its own night/morning image. It could not close a different piece. Earned.

Scores clear the bar; the REVISE is solely because the load-bearing historical claim the panel already flagged is still asserted as fact in three places — the hedge was applied to the wrong half of the sentence.

VERDICT: REVISE
TOP FIXES:
1. Hedge the CHILD claim, not just the evening-prayer claim: rework "And they were a child's prayer" / "Children learned it for the dark" / "the evening prayer" so no line asserts first-century children's nightly use as fact (e.g. "a bedtime prayer" + "words a child could learn for the dark" + "those evening words").
2. "Jewish tradition made Psalm 31 an evening prayer" → "made these words an evening prayer" (the tradition is verse 5, not the whole Psalm).
3. "Three days later, morning came" → "On the third day, morning came" (Luke 24:46's own wording; "three days later" overshoots to Monday).
