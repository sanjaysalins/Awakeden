The last words of Jesus on the cross (Luke 23:46) were not composed in the moment. They were a quotation, and by long Jewish tradition, an evening prayer: words a child could learn to say against the dark.

Psalm 31:5: "Into thine hand I commit my spirit: thou hast redeemed me, O LORD God of truth."

After the three hours of darkness had passed, Jesus prayed those evening words with his final breath: "And when Jesus had cried with a loud voice, he said, Father, into thy hands I commend my spirit: and having said thus, he gave up the ghost" (Luke 23:46, KJV).

But listen to the first word. Father. The Psalm does not have it. The dying Son adds it. Not judge. Not stranger. Father.

And the line he prayed says thou hast redeemed me - while on that very cross he was buying that redemption. He dies the way the prayer was meant to be prayed: like a child falling asleep in his father's arms. On the third day, morning came.

In this piece: the evening-prayer tradition behind Psalm 31:5, the one added word, the redemption clause inside the prayer, and why the resurrection turns a deathbed into a bedtime.
