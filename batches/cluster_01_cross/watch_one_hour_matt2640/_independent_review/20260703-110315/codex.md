# Independent review — codex (OK, 43s)

Hook score: 6/10. “The night before the cross…” is clear, but “asked his closest friends for one thing. Stay awake with me” is more explanatory than arresting.

Landing score: 7/10. “He watched that hour alone…” is built from the piece’s image, but “so you would never watch one alone” slightly overpromises from the text and softens the actual Gethsemane weight.

Findings:

- Doctrinal concern: “Then he went back to pray for the men who could not stay awake for him. Three times.” In Matthew 26, Jesus returns to pray to the Father about the cup and submission to the Father’s will. Saying he prayed “for the men” imports an intercessory claim not stated in this scene.
- Doctrinal/clarity concern: “Your salvation does not rest on your ability to stay awake for God. It rests on this. He stayed awake for you.” The contrast is strong, but “stayed awake for you” risks making the saving act sound like wakefulness rather than Christ’s obedient suffering, death, and resurrection. “All the way to the cross” helps, but the central image is still a little theologically thin.
- Overclaim: “Listen to the gentlest rebuke ever spoken.” “Ever” is unprovable and sounds like a freshness-chasing superlative.
- KJV: The scripture/Jesus lines appear verbatim KJV for Matthew 26:38, 26:40, and 26:41.
- Clarity: “Not a curse. A diagnosis full of mercy.” A listener can follow it, but “diagnosis” is abstract compared with the very concrete sleep/watch/pray thread.
- Grace framing is mostly strong: “Your salvation does not rest on your ability…” avoids works framing well.

VERDICT: REVISE
TOP FIXES:
1. Remove or correct “pray for the men,” because Matthew 26 does not say that.
2. Tighten “He stayed awake for you” so salvation rests explicitly on Christ’s obedient suffering and cross, not mere wakefulness.
3. Replace “gentlest rebuke ever spoken” with a text-grounded phrase that does not overclaim.
