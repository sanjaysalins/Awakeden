### Doctrinal soundness
**Mostly orthodox, but two lines overreach the text.**

1. **"He sees her wound. And he dresses it first."**  
   John 19:26–27 records Jesus *providing for* Mary — reassigning filial care to the beloved disciple. He does not dress or bind anything. Calling that act “dressing her wound” imports a medical metaphor the narrative never supports. Worse, **"first"** implies a priority order (Mary’s grief before atonement?) that the passage does not state. That is homiletic invention, not faithful depth.

2. **"While judgment fell on him, he was binding someone else's wound."**  
   Substitutionary judgment is orthodox in itself, but here it arrives **without setup** in a piece that never names whose judgment, on what basis, or for whom. A zero-Bible listener gets a second metaphor stack (“judgment” + “binding”) on top of the sword metaphor, with no anchor in the quoted verses.

3. **"Let him behold you"**  
   In the text, **behold** is Jesus’ command *to Mary* (“behold thy son”). Jesus *saw* his mother (v. 26); he did not command her to be beheld by him. Reversing the verb for a CTA is a wordplay move, not something John teaches — and it risks turning a unique historical moment into a generic “Jesus sees your pain” devotional.

No heresy; the problem is **metaphor piled on metaphor until the historical act (provision, adoption-in-death) disappears.**

### Clarity on first hearing
**Several beats lose a stranger.**

| Phrase | Problem |
|--------|---------|
| **"Thirty-three years later, the sword lands"** | “Thirty-three” is tradition, not cited text. “The sword lands” is vivid but ambiguous — literal weapon? Roman execution? Mary’s grief? |
| **"He dresses it first"** | “Dress a wound” is specialist English; many will hear “dresses *her*” or miss the idiom entirely. |
| **"He gave her John. A home. A son."** | **John is never named in the heard scripture.** The listener only heard “the disciple standing by, whom he loved.” The payoff name-drops without setup. |
| **"Your wound is not invisible to him"** | Abrupt jump from Mary’s sword to **your** wound with no bridge — what wound does the listener have? |
| **"Stand where Mary stood"** | Mary’s place at the cross is **unique** (mother of the crucified). Inviting the viewer to occupy it is poetic but logically muddy on first pass. |

The Simeon → cross thread is coherent for Bible-aware listeners; it is **not** clean for “zero Bible knowledge.”

### Freshness = faithful depth
**Mixed.** The Luke 2:35 → John 19 bridge is classic, faithful exegesis — not clickbait. The **surprise** is supposed to live in the text; instead the script’s freshest lines (**"dresses it first,"** **"binding someone else's wound,"** **"Let him behold you"**) are **narrator inventions** layered over the passage rather than discoveries *from* it. The actual textual shock — dying Jesus reorganizing family at the foot of the cross — is clearer in **"He gave her John. A home. A son."** than in the wound-dressing frame.

### One thread spine
**Mostly holds, weak at the landing.**  
Hook: Simeon’s sword → cross → Jesus sees Mary → cross words → provision.  
Middle application: wound / judgment / binding.  
CTA: stand as Mary / be beheld / bind what sword tore.

The spine frays when the CTA shifts from **Jesus providing a son for Mary** (textual) to **Jesus binding the viewer’s wounds** (generalized). Same vocabulary (sword, behold, bind), but **different subject and action** — thread-stretch, not thread-swap.

### Landing score: **7 / 10**
**"Stand where Mary stood. Let him behold you, and bind what the sword tore."**  
Not a stock closer (“come to Jesus” family) — **"sword"** and **"behold"** tie to this piece. But **"Let him behold you"** could end several cross-pieces about being seen by Christ; the Mary-specific provision thread is abandoned. **"Bind what the sword tore"** uses **tore**, while KJV says **pierce** — narrator drift from the piece’s own Scripture image. Could this close another piece? Not generically — but the **behold** inversion is reusable cross-cluster language. Earned, not stock; still not “in the bones” of *Behold thy son / Behold thy mother*.

**Both scores under 8 → REVISE per your rubric.**

### Strongest line (for calibration)
**"That is who is dying there."** — Christological, text-grounded, lands the middle without fear framing. The piece is closest to excellence when it describes what Jesus **did** (gave a home, a son), not what the narrator **reimagines** (dressing wounds).

VERDICT: REVISE
TOP FIXES:
1. Drop or rewrite **"He sees her wound. And he dresses it first"** and **"binding someone else's wound"** — stay on what John actually shows: Jesus sees Mary and *provides* (son, home, care); remove **"first"** priority over atonement.
2. Rebuild the landing from **"Woman, behold thy son!" / "Behold thy mother!"** — provision, being seen, family given in the hour of death — not **"Stand where Mary stood / Let him behold you"** (Mary role-play + reversed behold).
3. Fix the John beat for first-time listeners: either name him when scripture introduces **"the disciple… whom he loved"** or cut **"John"** and say **"He gave her the disciple — a home, a son."**
